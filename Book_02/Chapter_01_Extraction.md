## 1. Chapter Thesis

A penalty shootout is "a merciless beast" that can, in a split second, turn superstars into scapegoats —
because the simple skill of a penalty kick (which elite players convert 19-or-20 times out of 20 in
relaxed training) collapses once you add a crowd, a TV audience, and consequences. This chapter maps how
pressure is *felt* and how it makes players **choke**. Acute stress sets off a universal
psychophysiological cascade (cortisol, tunnel vision, narrowed attention, degraded fine-motor control);
anxiety is near-universal among takers but *dynamic*, peaking in the powerless waiting of the centre
circle. From there the chapter catalogues the mechanisms of failure: **overthinking** (self-focus that
disrupts an automatic skill), **ironic processes** ("don't miss" making a miss more likely), **quiet-eye
disruption and distraction**, **avoidance gaze** (looking away for relief), and **speeding up** (treating
the whistle as a starting gun to end the dread). Two opposite errors emerge — too little attention to the
process (rushing) *and* too much (overthinking) — and pressure bites hardest on "negative shots," where a
miss means instant defeat (scored only 63% of the time vs. 89% when a goal means instant victory). The
shootout is a mental game, and how you emerge from the beast depends on whether you cope or choke.

## 2. Key Concepts

```
Concept Name: Choking Under Pressure
Definition: Performing worse than your established skill level precisely because the stakes are high — the
opposite of clutch performance.
Why It Matters: It is the chapter's central phenomenon; the penalty (easy in training, hard on the world
stage) is a near-perfect natural instance, and football "approaches the condition of an individual sport"
at the spot.
How the Author Uses It: The umbrella for the whole chapter; distinguished from a miss caused by a good
save, bad luck, or plain lack of skill (a miss is not always a choke). Crucially, choking is easy to
identify in *individual* sports (van de Velde, Novotná, Cate Campbell) but ambiguous in *team* sports (AC
Milan 2005, Bayern 1999, Brazil 2014 might be opponent excellence, not choking) — which is exactly why the
penalty, where football "approaches the condition of an individual sport," is the clean choking laboratory.
Related Concepts: Overthinking, ironic processes, distraction, quiet eye, acute stress.
```

```
Concept Name: Acute Stress and the Psychophysiological Cascade
Definition: The body's automatic threat response — cortisol release, dilated pupils, blurred peripheral
vision ("tunnel vision"), altered hearing, and attention funnelled onto the stressor, impairing working
memory, cognitive flexibility, and fine motor control.
Why It Matters: It is the raw material of choking and a *universal* human response (penalty spot, opening
night, a wedding speech) — some effects help (less distractible), others backfire (worse judgment, more
risk-taking, shakier hands).
How the Author Uses It: To ground the felt experience of pressure in physiology; even *spectators* suffer
(heart-attack spikes).
Related Concepts: Threat vs. challenge appraisal, tunnel vision, quiet-eye reduction.
```

```
Concept Name: Anxiety as Dynamic (not stable)
Definition: Shootout anxiety is near-universal among takers but constantly evolving — coming and going
with events — rather than a fixed state; for many it peaks in the centre circle (waiting, watching,
powerless) and eases once their own performance begins on the walk.
Why It Matters: It reframes "nerves" as a moving target that can be managed, and locates the worst of it
in the phase of least control.
How the Author Uses It: The Euro 2004 interview findings; sets up the coping strategies of Chapter 2.
Related Concepts: Perceived control, powerlessness, the walk, threat appraisal.
```

```
Concept Name: Overthinking (Self-Focus) Theory of Choking
Definition: Performance anxiety turns attention *inward* to consciously controlling and monitoring
movements that are normally automatic; "what usually is smooth, natural and effortless becomes measured,
restrained, complicated."
Why It Matters: One of the two great mechanisms of choking; explains why an expert's skill degrades under
scrutiny (and why novices, conversely, benefit from skill-focus).
How the Author Uses It: Rashford's record 11-second pause and Rapinoe's longest-of-20 pause as candidate
cases; grounded in Baumeister (1984) and fNIRS brain data.
Related Concepts: Explicit monitoring, "you have to be perfect," speeding up (the opposite error).
```

```
Concept Name: Ironic Processes
Definition: Instructing yourself *not* to do something, under load, increases the probability of doing
exactly that — "don't miss, don't miss, DON'T MISS!" makes a miss more likely.
Why It Matters: A specific, counterintuitive trap for penalty takers, whose desire not to miss is intense.
How the Author Uses It: The Free University of Amsterdam studies — players told not to shoot within the
goalkeeper's reach looked at and shot toward the goalkeeper *more*.
Related Concepts: Overthinking, attention, Wegner's ironic mental control.
```

```
Concept Name: Quiet Eye (and its disruption)
Definition: The duration of the final visual fixation on a relevant target before the final movement
begins; longer quiet eye predicts better performance across 30+ studies, and pressure tends to shorten
it.
Why It Matters: A measurable gaze signature of good performance under pressure — and a route by which
anxiety (via distraction) degrades it.
How the Author Uses It: Sophia Smith's darting gaze (miss) vs. the three US takers' locked-on routines
(goals); basketball free-throws dropping 68%→57% under pressure.
Related Concepts: Distraction theory of choking, threat vs. challenge, avoidance gaze.
```

```
Concept Name: Avoidance Gaze / Approach vs. Avoidance Looking
Definition: After placing the ball, a taker either walks back facing the goalkeeper ("approach looking")
or turns their back to the goalkeeper ("avoidance looking") for relief from the confrontation.
Why It Matters: It reveals pressure (turning away rose to 44% on the highest-pressure shots) and national
differences (57% of English takers), and separates *strategic* disengagement (adaptive, control-taking)
from *reactive* avoidance (relief that isn't enough).
How the Author Uses It: Paul Ince's reactive turn-and-miss vs. Lewandowski's rehearsed turn-away
(forwards who avoid strategically score 87% vs. 70%).
Related Concepts: Strategic disengagement (ch. 3), perceptual/experiential avoidance, "you cannot not
communicate."
```

```
Concept Name: Speeding Up (the whistle as a starting gun)
Definition: Under pressure, footballers treat the referee's whistle — which *grants permission* to kick —
as an instruction to kick *now*, rushing to end the dread; English takers averaged 0.28 s reaction, and
takers generally reacted in under 1 s where other sports take 6–30 s.
Why It Matters: One of the two ways to choke — too little attention to the process; players who instantly
ran scored <60% vs. >80% for those who paused a moment.
How the Author Uses It: Carragher's false start; the English "get it over with" quotes; the dread-of-
anticipation research.
Related Concepts: Overthinking (the opposite error), the two ways to choke, negative shots.
```

```
Concept Name: The Two Ways to Choke (process attention)
Definition: Both too little attention to the process (a very short pause — rushing) and too much (a very
long pause — overthinking) are linked to under-performance; the goal is not "pause long" but *what you do*
with the time.
Why It Matters: It resolves the apparent contradiction between "speeding up is bad" and "long pauses are
bad" into a single U-shaped principle.
How the Author Uses It: Rashford's 11 s (too much) vs. Carragher's rush (too little); Mbappé/Griezmann's
short-but-routine pauses show short isn't always bad.
Related Concepts: Overthinking, speeding up, pre-performance routine (ch. 2).
```

```
Concept Name: Shot Importance and the "Negative Shot"
Definition: A "negative shot" is one where a miss instantly loses the game and a goal merely continues the
shootout — defined by its potential negative consequence; the prospect of losing suppresses performance
while the prospect of winning sustains it.
Why It Matters: It quantifies how stakes shape outcome: negative shots are scored 63%, "positive" shots
(a goal wins instantly) 89%, neutral shots 72%.
How the Author Uses It: Baggio's 1994 World Cup final miss ("a kick to save the World Cup"); the elite/
domestic replication caveats.
Related Concepts: Loss aversion, pressure, in-game vs. shootout conversion.
```

## 3. Key Claims

```
Claim: A missed penalty is often a choke, but not always.
Type: Conceptual / Empirical
Evidence Provided: In relaxed elite training almost everyone scores (19–20 of 20); adding crowd, TV, and
consequences makes "the odds for choking skyrocket." But a miss can also be a great save, bad luck, or an
inexplicable lapse of skill with anxiety playing little role (Berg's view of Beckham).
Strength of Support: Strong as a framing distinction; the training observation is the author's first-hand
report.
```

```
Claim: 53% of players who miss display shrinking / shame behaviour.
Type: Empirical (descriptive)
Evidence Provided: Jordet's corpus of all 718 shots from every men's World Cup, Euros, and Champions
League shootout, 1970–2023: 53% of missers make themselves look smaller, fall to the ground, hide their
faces, or look down and avoid teammates.
Strength of Support: Strong (large, exhaustive descriptive sample); descriptive, not causal.
```

```
Claim: Even watching a shootout is physically dangerous.
Type: Empirical (epidemiological)
Evidence Provided: Munich cardiovascular hospital admissions tripled (≈20 → 64) on the day Germany's 2006
World Cup quarter-final went to penalties; the Netherlands' 1996 Euro penalty loss to France coincided
with 14 more heart-attack/stroke deaths than normal; 20+ studies worldwide document similar effects.
Strength of Support: Moderate to Strong; multiple studies, but correlational (event-day association).
```

```
Claim: Anxiety is near-universal but dynamic, and peaks in the centre circle.
Type: Empirical (interview) / Theoretical
Evidence Provided: In the Euro 2004 study, anxiety was the only one of 24 emotions all 10 players
reported; it came and went with events (one player calmed once a teammate missed), and several felt it
most acutely while waiting powerlessly in the centre circle ("it is not in your hands"). Consistent with
theories that anxiety peaks just before competition onset and dissipates once it begins.
Strength of Support: Moderate (small n=10, retrospective self-report) but theory-consistent.
```

```
Claim: Overthinking (self-focus) disrupts the automatic skill and causes choking.
Type: Theoretical / Empirical
Evidence Provided: Baumeister (1984), six experiments linking pressure, self-consciousness, and choking;
a dribbling study where experts are harmed by step-by-step self-monitoring while novices benefit; Dutch
fNIRS data showing anxious experienced players had higher left-temporal-cortex activation (self-
instruction/reflection) — they "stopped executing automatically and started thinking." Candidate cases:
Rashford's 11-second pause and "you have to be perfect"; Rapinoe's longest-of-20 pause.
Strength of Support: Strong for the theory; the Rashford/Rapinoe attributions are explicitly flagged as
speculative (tiny margins, possible coincidence).
```

```
Claim: Ironic processes make "don't miss" backfire.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Free University of Amsterdam studies — experienced players told *not* to shoot within
the goalkeeper's reach ended up looking at and shooting toward the goalkeeper more than players told to
aim for open areas.
Strength of Support: Moderate to Strong (lab studies, converging with the broader ironic-process
literature).
```

```
Claim: Longer quiet eye predicts better performance; pressure shortens it — but mindset moderates.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: 30+ studies show a positive effect of longer quiet eye across sports; basketball
free-throws fell 68%→57% under pressure as quiet-eye duration dropped; those who appraise the situation as
a *challenge* keep longer quiet eye than those who feel *threatened*.
Strength of Support: Strong (large, cross-sport literature); the football-specific gaze evidence is
mixed/correlational.
```

```
Claim: There are two opposite ways to choke — rushing and overthinking.
Type: Empirical / Theoretical
Evidence Provided: Players who instantly ran after the whistle scored <60% vs. >80% for those who paused
(2009 study, 37 shootouts / 366 kicks); yet the longest pauses also under-perform (Rashford). Recent data
(past 3 years, men and women): longest-time takers scored 78% vs. 51% for the shortest; in the 2020
Olympics all five shootouts were won by the team with the longer average post-whistle time.
Strength of Support: Strong; a clean U-shaped account with the caveat that short pauses aren't always bad
(Mbappé, Griezmann) — "the critical component is what players do with the time."
```

```
Claim: Stakes shape outcome — negative shots are missed more.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Across major shootouts, "negative shots" (miss = instant loss) are scored 63%,
"positive" shots (goal = instant win) 89%, neutral shots 72%; in-game penalties convert ~4% higher than
the same takers' shootout penalties (French Cup: 76% vs. 73.1%). The prospect of *losing* suppresses
performance; the prospect of *winning* sustains it.
Strength of Support: Moderate to Strong; replicated in a French-cup study (56% at maximum stakes) but
*not* in a 115-competition sample — Jordet notes his elite-tournament sample may differ from lower-level
domestic shootouts.
```

## 4. Frameworks, Models, and Mental Models

```
Name: The Two Ways to Choke (a U-shaped process-attention model)
Description: Under pressure, both extremes of pre-shot timing signal choking.
Components: Too little attention to the process (a very short, rushed pause) → under-performance;
too much attention (a very long pause / overthinking) → under-performance; a "good enough," routine-
based middle.
How It Works: Rushing = treating the whistle as a starting gun to end dread; overthinking = explicit
self-monitoring that disrupts the automatic skill. What matters is *what you do* with the time, not merely
its length.
When It Is Useful: Diagnosing pressure failures; designing pre-performance routines (ch. 2).
Limitations: Pause length is a coarse proxy; short pauses can be optimal for some (Mbappé, Griezmann);
correlation ≠ causation.
```

```
Name: Threat vs. Challenge Appraisal
Description: The same stressful situation is read by some as threatening (negative) and by others as
challenging (positive), with opposite performance consequences.
Components: A stressor; an appraisal (threat or challenge); downstream effects on quiet eye and coping.
How It Works: Threat appraisal → shorter quiet eye, more distraction, worse performance; challenge
appraisal → longer quiet eye, better performance. "With the proper mindset, the negative effects from
pressure can be countered."
When It Is Useful: The hinge between the "feeling" (ch. 1) and "controlling" (ch. 2) the pressure.
Limitations: Introduced here as a moderator; the *how* of reappraisal is deferred to later chapters.
```

```
Name: Approach vs. Avoidance Looking (and Strategic Disengagement)
Description: Two ways to walk back from the ball, and the distinction between deliberate and reactive
turning-away.
Components: Approach looking (face the goalkeeper) vs. avoidance looking (back to the goalkeeper);
strategic disengagement (rehearsed, control-taking) vs. reactive avoidance (relief-seeking, distracting).
How It Works: Turning away rises with pressure (44% on must-score shots) and expectation (57% of English
takers); strategic turners (forwards like Lewandowski) score more (87% vs. 70%), while reactive turners
(Paul Ince) get only temporary relief before confronting the still-present fear.
When It Is Useful: Reading a taker's coping; distinguishing helpful from harmful gaze behaviour.
Limitations: The binary "back or not" coding is crude (a finer Bundesliga head-direction study found
away/down gaze linked to more misses); no absolutes, correlation ≠ causation.
```

## 5. Research and Evidence

```
Study / Research: The 718-kick descriptive video corpus
Researchers: Geir Jordet
Year: Covers 1970–2023
Research Question: How do players behave — especially after missing — across the full history of elite
men's shootouts?
Method: Video coding of every shot (718) from every men's World Cup, European Championship, and Champions
League penalty shootout since 1970.
Key Finding: 53% of missers display shrinking/shame behaviour (look smaller, fall, hide face, avoid
teammates).
How the Author Uses It: The empirical backbone for "the shootout is a merciless beast" and later
behavioural analyses.
Important Limitations: Descriptive/correlational; men only.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: None; it is the author's own corpus.
```

```
Study / Research: The Euro 2004 emotion interviews
Researchers: Geir Jordet and colleagues
Year: 2004 onward
Research Question: What do penalty takers feel, and how does it evolve, during a real shootout?
Method: Interviews with all 10 players from the Sweden–Netherlands quarter-final; a checklist of 24
emotions (14 positive, 10 negative).
Key Finding: Anxiety was the only emotion all 10 reported; it was dynamic (came/went with events) and
often peaked in the centre circle; determination was the most common positive emotion (8 players).
How the Author Uses It: To show anxiety is universal but manageable and phase-dependent.
Important Limitations: Small (n=10), retrospective self-report.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: Framed as consistent with sport-anxiety theory (anxiety peaks
pre-onset).
```

```
Study / Research: Overthinking / self-focus theory of choking
Researchers: Roy Baumeister (1984); later dribbling studies; Dutch fNIRS brain researchers
Year: 1984 onward
Research Question: Does pressure-induced self-consciousness disrupt automatic skill?
Method: Baumeister — six experiments on pressure, self-consciousness, and performance; a ball-dribbling
study contrasting experts and novices; fNIRS optical brain monitoring of haemoglobin across brain areas
during penalty kicks.
Key Finding: Pressure and self-consciousness lower expert performance (novices benefit from skill-focus);
anxious experienced players show higher left-temporal-cortex activation (self-instruction/reflection) —
they think about a skill they'd normally run automatically.
How the Author Uses It: The mechanism behind Rashford's and Rapinoe's long-pause misses.
Important Limitations: The football brain evidence is correlational; applying it to specific misses is
speculative.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: A well-established literature "elaborated on" over decades.
```

```
Study / Research: Ironic processes in penalty kicking
Researchers: A research group at the Free University of Amsterdam
Year: Not specified.
Research Question: Are penalty takers vulnerable to ironic (do-the-thing-you're-avoiding) effects?
Method: Lab kicks by experienced players under different instructions (e.g., "don't shoot within the
goalkeeper's reach" vs. aim for open areas).
Key Finding: Players told not to shoot near the goalkeeper looked at and shot toward the goalkeeper more.
How the Author Uses It: To explain why the intense wish "not to miss" can cause a miss.
Important Limitations: Laboratory setting (the artificial-pressure limitation the Introduction flagged).
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: Converges with the broader ironic-mental-control literature.
```

```
Study / Research: Quiet eye and pressure
Researchers: Various (30+ studies); a basketball free-throw study
Year: Not specified.
Research Question: Does the final fixation before movement predict performance, and does pressure disrupt
it?
Method: Eye-tracking across many sport tasks; a free-throw study contrasting low- and high-pressure
conversion and quiet-eye duration.
Key Finding: Longer quiet eye → better performance; pressure shortened it and dropped free-throw
conversion 68%→57%; challenge (vs. threat) appraisal preserves quiet eye.
How the Author Uses It: Links gaze to the distraction theory of choking; Sophia Smith's darting eyes.
Important Limitations: Football-specific gaze effects are mixed (Rapinoe had steady gaze and still
missed); "no absolutes."
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: A large, convergent cross-sport literature.
```

```
Study / Research: Avoidance gaze and national differences
Researchers: Geir Jordet (2008; 2009 "Why do English players fail in soccer penalty shootouts?"); a later
Bundesliga head-direction study (others)
Year: 2008, 2009; Bundesliga study covers 2011–2016
Research Question: When and why do takers turn away from the goalkeeper, and does it matter?
Method: Video coding of major-tournament shootouts (turn-back vs. not) by pressure level and country; the
Bundesliga study coded finer head direction over 400 kicks.
Key Finding: Turning away rose to 44% on must-score shots (vs. 14% must-win, 30% neutral); 57% of English
takers turned away (highest of 8 countries). Overall, avoidance (72%) vs. approach (74%) scoring differs
negligibly — but forwards who *strategically* turn away score 87% vs. 70%, and the finer Bundesliga coding
found away/down gaze linked to more misses. Lab studies: goalkeepers feel more confident and perform
better against takers who look away.
How the Author Uses It: To separate strategic disengagement from reactive avoidance, and to explain
English underperformance (expectation, the 1966 gap, media).
Important Limitations: Binary coding is crude; correlation ≠ causation; behaviour has since changed
(post-2008 hardly anyone turns away).
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: Jordet reports the later study's critique of his binary measure and
accepts it.
```

```
Study / Research: Speeding up and post-whistle pause
Researchers: Geir Jordet (2009 study; earlier pre-2007 timing data; recent 3-year analysis)
Year: 2009; recent (past 3 years)
Research Question: Does rushing after the whistle hurt performance?
Method: Coding reaction time to the whistle across all major men's shootouts to 2009 (37 shootouts, 366
kicks) and, recently, men's and women's shootouts.
Key Finding: Instant runners scored <60% vs. >80% for those who paused; English averaged 0.28 s (vs.
Bolt's 0.15 s reaction, but far quicker than other takers/sports — NBA ~6 s, rugby 10–15 s, golf 20–30
s). Recently, longest-time takers scored 78% vs. 51% for the shortest; the 2020 Olympics' five shootouts
were all won by the longer-pausing team. Average post-whistle pause more than doubled to >2 s over the
2010s.
How the Author Uses It: One of the two ways to choke; the media/Ronaldo-Neymar shift toward pausing.
Important Limitations: Pause length is a proxy; short can be optimal (Mbappé, Griezmann); "what you do
with the time" matters more.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: Widely picked up by media ("tortoises fare better than hares").
```

```
Study / Research: Shot importance / negative shots
Researchers: Geir Jordet (major-tournament analyses); a French-cup replication; a 115-competition sample
(others)
Year: Not specified.
Research Question: Does the stake of a shot change its success rate?
Method: Classifying every shootout kick by consequence (negative / positive / neutral) and comparing
conversion; comparing in-game vs. shootout conversion.
Key Finding: Negative shots (miss = instant loss) scored 63%; positive shots (goal = instant win) 89%;
neutral 72%. In-game penalties convert ~4% higher than the same takers' shootout kicks (French Cup 76% vs.
73.1%). Replicated in a French-cup study (56% at maximum stakes) but *not* in a 115-competition, 11-year
sample.
How the Author Uses It: To show the prospect of losing suppresses performance while winning sustains it;
Baggio's 1994 miss.
Important Limitations: Mixed replication; Jordet's elite-tournament sample may not generalize to
lower-level domestic shootouts.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: Explicitly — one replication, one non-replication.
```

## 6. Experiments

```
Experiment Name: Baumeister's choking experiments (1984)
Setup: Manipulating pressure and self-consciousness during skilled performance.
Participants: Not specified (lab participants).
Procedure: Six experiments varying pressure/self-focus and measuring performance.
Result: Pressure and heightened self-consciousness lowered performance (choking).
Interpretation: Self-focus disrupts automatically-run skills — the founding evidence for overthinking
theory.
What It Demonstrates: Choking has an attentional-self-focus mechanism.
Potential Alternative Explanation: Distraction (not self-focus) as an alternative choking mechanism —
which the chapter also presents (quiet eye).
```

```
Experiment Name: The ironic-instruction penalty study (Free University of Amsterdam)
Setup: Experienced footballers taking lab penalties under different verbal instructions.
Participants: Experienced football players.
Procedure: Some told *not* to shoot within the goalkeeper's reach; others told to shoot accurately / at
open areas.
Result: The "don't shoot near the keeper" group looked at and shot toward the goalkeeper more.
Interpretation: Negation-framed goals backfire under load — an ironic process.
What It Demonstrates: How the wish "don't miss" can produce the miss.
Potential Alternative Explanation: Lab pressure is weaker than real shootout pressure (a limitation the
book itself stresses).
```

```
Experiment Name: The quiet-eye free-throw study
Setup: Basketball free-throw shooters under low vs. high pressure.
Participants: Free-throw shooters.
Procedure: Compare conversion and quiet-eye duration across pressure conditions.
Result: Conversion fell 68%→57% and quiet-eye duration dropped significantly under pressure.
Interpretation: Pressure shortens the final fixation (distraction), degrading performance.
What It Demonstrates: A measurable gaze mechanism linking pressure to failure.
Potential Alternative Explanation: Threat vs. challenge appraisal moderates who suffers the reduction.
```

## 7. Cases and Stories

```
Case Title: The raw early shootouts — Kempes, Rix, and the rushing 1984 final
People / Organization: Mario Kempes, Graham Rix (1980 Cup Winners' Cup, Arsenal–Valencia); Gary Stevens,
Enzo Scifo (1984 UEFA Cup, Tottenham–Anderlecht)
Context: The chapter opens with the FIFA-adopted format's first, unpolished shootouts (1970 onward), when
takers "had not learned about how one should or shouldn't behave."
What Happened: In the first major European club final to go to penalties (1980), Mario Kempes — a 1978
World Cup winner and superstar — missed, "the first time the world had seen one of the game's living
legends get stripped of their dignity" by a shootout; Graham Rix then missed too, and Arsenal became the
first team to lose a European final on penalties. In the 1984 UEFA Cup final, nine of ten takers reacted
to the whistle "as if it were the crack of a starting pistol"; Gary Stevens even kicked *simultaneously*
with the whistle, while 18-year-old Enzo Scifo's composed one-second pause "feels like an eternity"
compared with the rest.
Outcome: Establishes both the superstar liability (Kempes) and the rushing tendency (the whistle as a
starting gun) from the format's earliest days.
Concept Illustrated: The superstar liability; speeding up; the unrehearsed rawness of early shootouts.
Why This Case Is Useful: A historical origin for two of the chapter's core themes in one passage.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: Stielike and Six — the first World Cup shootout (1982)
People / Organization: Uli Stielike, Harald Schumacher, Pierre Littbarski (West Germany); Didier Six
(France)
Context: The 1982 World Cup semi-final in Seville — the first shootout ever used to decide a World Cup
game — after an exhausting 3-3 (France had led 3-1 in extra time).
What Happened: Stielike missed (the first German ever to miss a major-tournament penalty — it wouldn't
happen again for 34 years), then curled into "a tight ball of pain" by the spot for 10 seconds until
Schumacher lifted him and Littbarski consoled him. Didier Six also missed and curled up in the six-yard
box; his trauma outlived the match — "I had difficulty finding a job, because they said, 'That one is
unstable.'"
Outcome: The world learned the shootout is "a merciless beast" that turns superstars into scapegoats.
Concept Illustrated: Post-miss trauma/shame; acute stress; the ruthlessness of the format.
Why This Case Is Useful: The founding, unvarnished image of shootout pain.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Marcus Rashford's 11-second pause (Euro 2020 final)
People / Organization: Marcus Rashford (England); Gianluigi Donnarumma (Italy)
Context: The Wembley final shootout vs. Italy.
What Happened: Rashford stood still for 11 seconds after the whistle — the longest pause in all 718 elite
kicks coded (1976–today). He felt "something was off," his brain said "you have to be perfect," and he
tried a new stuttering run-up; he hesitated and hit the post. England lost (Sancho and Saka also missed).
On his previous 10 penalties he paused ~5.7 s and scored all 10.
Outcome: A textbook candidate for an overthinking choke.
Concept Illustrated: Overthinking / self-focus; "you have to be perfect"; changing your routine under
pressure.
Why This Case Is Useful: A precise, quantified anomaly (his own baseline vs. the record pause).
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Megan Rapinoe's longest-of-20 pause (2023 Women's World Cup)
People / Organization: Megan Rapinoe (USA)
Context: The Sweden shootout — her last appearance before international retirement, huge expectation.
What Happened: She paused 5.7 s (longer than any of her last 20 penalties) and blasted the ball high over
the bar — her first miss in 16 kicks and first time missing the goal entirely in 21.
Outcome: A second quantified candidate for an overthinking choke, in the women's game.
Concept Illustrated: Overthinking; the weight of a career-defining moment.
Why This Case Is Useful: Extends the overthinking pattern beyond one player/one gender.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Marcelo holding on (2014 World Cup; 2011–12 Champions League)

## 9. Counterintuitive Insights

```
Insight: A penalty is an easy skill that becomes hard only because of pressure.
Common Belief: Missing a penalty means the taker lacked the skill.
Author's Argument: Elite players convert ~19/20 in relaxed training; the crowd, cameras, and consequences
— not the skill — are what make them miss.
Evidence: First-hand elite training observations; the ~80% in-game vs. lower shootout conversion.
Why It Is Surprising: It relocates failure from ability to context.
```

```
Insight: Choking is obvious in individual sports but ambiguous in team sports.
Common Belief: A big-stage collapse (AC Milan 2005, Brazil 2014) is obviously choking.
Author's Argument: In team sports, a collapse may reflect the opponent's excellence (Liverpool, Germany)
rather than choking — only in individual sports (or the penalty, where football "approaches an individual
sport") can you cleanly isolate it.
Evidence: van de Velde, Novotná, Cate Campbell (clear); AC Milan, Bayern, Brazil (ambiguous).
Why It Is Surprising: The most dramatic "chokes" may not be chokes at all — which is why the lone penalty
taker is the ideal case.
```

```
Insight: Telling yourself "don't miss" makes you more likely to miss.
Common Belief: Focusing hard on not failing helps you not fail.
Author's Argument: Under load, negation-framed goals ironically increase the avoided behaviour.
Evidence: The Free University of Amsterdam studies (looked/shot toward the keeper more).
Why It Is Surprising: The very intensity of not wanting to miss becomes the cause of missing.
```

```
Insight: There are two opposite ways to choke.
Common Belief: Choking is one thing — "bottling it."
Author's Argument: Too little attention to the process (rushing) and too much (overthinking) both cause
under-performance; the middle is a routine you trust.
Evidence: Instant runners <60% vs. paused >80%; yet the longest pauses (Rashford) also miss.
Why It Is Surprising: The fix for one error (rush → pause) can cause the other (pause → overthink).

```

Author's Argument: *Strategic* disengagement (Lewandowski) is control-taking and scores more (87% vs.

Author's Argument: "Negative shots" (miss = loss) are scored 63%, but "positive shots" (goal = win) 89% —

Why It Is Surprising: An asymmetry (loss aversion) shows up in a physical skill's success rate.
```

```
Insight: Footballers rush a shot in under a second that other athletes take up to 30 seconds over.
Common Belief: Players take their time to focus before a big kick.
Author's Argument: Under dread, the whistle (permission to kick) is misread as an instruction to kick

## 11. Tensions, Contradictions, and Open Questions

```
Issue: Is a given miss a choke, or just a miss?
Author's Position: Penalty performance is largely about pressure; overthinking/rushing/avoidance are
plausible mechanisms.
Possible Counterargument: The margins are tiny and the cognitive mechanisms are "inevitably speculative";
some misses are coincidences, great saves, or plain lapses (Berg on Beckham). Jordet concedes the
Rashford/Rapinoe attributions are speculation.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Real-time psychophysiological measurement at the spot (which, Jordet
notes, has not yet been done).
```

```
Issue: Avoidance gaze — does it actually matter?
Author's Position: Strategic disengagement helps; reactive avoidance can hurt.
Possible Counterargument: Overall avoidance vs. approach scoring differs negligibly (72% vs. 74%); the
binary coding is crude; correlation ≠ causation — "I would never dream of telling Lewandowski to stop."
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Finer head-direction coding (the Bundesliga study) and causal/
intervention designs.
```

```
Issue: The negative-shot effect doesn't always replicate.
Author's Position: Negative shots are scored less (63%), reflecting the burden of the prospect of losing.
Possible Counterargument: Replicated in a French-cup study but *not* in a 115-competition, 11-year sample;
Jordet notes his elite-tournament sample may not generalize to lower-level domestic shootouts.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Consistent effect estimates across levels of play, controlling for
sample and stakes.
```

```
Issue: Longer pause = better, but not always.
Author's Position: Recent data show longer post-whistle times score better (78% vs. 51%).
Possible Counterargument: The longest pauses also under-perform (overthinking); short pauses are optimal
for some (Mbappé, Griezmann). Pause length is a proxy for "what you do with the time," not a lever to pull.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Measures of *how* the pause is used (the ch. 2 coping content), not
just its duration.
```

## 13. Psychology Connections

- **Choking under pressure.** The chapter's spine, with two mechanisms — self-focus/overthinking
  (Baumeister) and distraction (quiet eye) — presented as complementary.
- **Acute stress physiology.** Cortisol, tunnel vision, narrowed attention, degraded fine-motor control;
  and even spectator morbidity (heart-attack spikes).
- **Anxiety, appraisal, and control.** Anxiety as dynamic and phase-dependent; threat vs. challenge
  appraisal as the moderator; powerlessness in the centre circle as the peak.
- **Ironic mental control.** Wegner-style rebound — "don't miss" producing the miss.
- **Attention and gaze (quiet eye).** The final fixation as a performance signature; distraction theory.
- **Experiential/perceptual avoidance.** Looking away to escape distress — and its limits for an active
  performer.
- **Loss aversion / framing.** The negative-shot asymmetry (loss looms larger than gain).
- **Temporal discounting of dread.** Preferring pain now to anticipated pain later (the shock study) —
  why players rush.

## 14. Mathematics and Decision Science Connections

- **Base rates and conversion asymmetries.** 80% in-game vs. lower shootout rates; 63/89/72% by shot
  type; 78% vs. 51% by pause length — a chapter rich in comparative percentages.
- **Loss aversion.** The negative-shot effect is a behavioural-economics asymmetry surfacing in a motor
  skill.
- **Replication and generalizability.** Jordet explicitly reports one replication and one non-replication
  of the negative-shot effect and flags his elite-sample scope — good evidentiary hygiene.
- **U-shaped (inverted) performance curve.** The "two ways to choke" is a non-monotonic dose–response
  between process-attention (pause length) and performance.

**Inferred (mine):** correlation-vs-causation caveats recur (avoidance gaze, pause length) — the data are
observational, so effects are associations, echoing the causal-inference limits flagged in the
Introduction.

## 15. Sports Connections

**Direct (this is a sports book):** The entire chapter is elite football — dozens of named shootouts and
takers (Kempes, Rix, Stielike, Six, Rashford, Rapinoe, Baggio, Carragher, Lewandowski, Ince, etc.);
cross-sport pressure data (basketball free-throws 68%→57%; NBA ~6 s, rugby 10–15 s, golf 20–30 s pre-shot
times; Usain Bolt's 0.15 s reaction); and individual-sport choking exemplars (van de Velde 1999 British
Open, Novotná 1993 Wimbledon, Cate Campbell 2016 Olympics).

**Inferred (mine):** the mechanisms generalize to any discrete high-pressure skill — the free throw, the
field-goal kick, the service toss, the putt, the closing pitch. "Quiet eye," pre-performance routines,
the two-ways-to-choke curve, and threat-vs-challenge appraisal are cross-sport performance principles;
the negative-shot asymmetry maps onto any win-or-lose single action.

## 16. AI and Machine Learning Connections

**Direct from the book:** None identified.

**Inferred (mine):**
- **Behavioural video analytics.** Coding 718 kicks (pause length, gaze/head direction, post-miss body
  language) prefigures computer-vision pipelines (pose estimation, gaze tracking, temporal segmentation)
  for automated performance analysis.
- **Automaticity vs. explicit control.** The overthinking mechanism — a well-trained automatic policy
  degraded by conscious step-by-step monitoring — rhymes with over-deliberation / distribution shift when
  a learned controller is forced off-policy.
- **Observational causal inference.** The chapter's repeated correlation-≠-causation caveats and mixed
  replications are a case study in the perils of drawing causal claims from observational sport data
  (confounding, selection).
- **Reward framing.** The negative-shot asymmetry parallels how loss-vs-gain reward framing changes an
  agent's behaviour under the same objective.

## 19. Chapter Summary for Cross-Book Comparison

```
Main Thesis: A penalty shootout is "a merciless beast": the simple penalty skill (converted ~19/20 in
relaxed training) collapses under crowd, cameras, and consequences, and this chapter maps how pressure is
felt and how it makes players choke. Acute stress triggers a universal psychophysiological cascade;
anxiety is near-universal but dynamic, peaking in the powerless centre circle. Choking runs through
distinct mechanisms — overthinking (self-focus), ironic processes ("don't miss"), quiet-eye disruption/
distraction, avoidance gaze, and speeding up — yielding two opposite errors (too little vs. too much
attention to the process). Stakes bite hardest on "negative shots," where a miss means instant defeat
(scored 63% vs. 89% when a goal means instant victory).

Top 5 Concepts:
1. Choking under pressure (an easy skill wrecked by stakes)
2. Acute stress / psychophysiology of pressure (and spectator morbidity)
3. Overthinking (self-focus) and its opposite, speeding up — "the two ways to choke"
4. Quiet eye / distraction and avoidance gaze (strategic vs. reactive)
5. The negative shot (loss looms larger than gain)

Top 3 Claims:
1. Two opposite ways to choke — rushing (<60% scored) and overthinking (longest pauses also miss);
   longest-time takers now score 78% vs. 51% for the shortest.
2. Negative shots (miss = instant loss) are scored 63% vs. 89% for positive shots (goal = instant win).
3. Overthinking disrupts the automatic skill (Baumeister 1984; fNIRS; Rashford/Rapinoe), and "don't miss"
   backfires via ironic processes.

Top 3 Cases:
1. Stielike and Six — the first World Cup shootout (1982) and the enduring trauma of a miss
2. Rashford's record 11-second pause (Euro 2020) — the overthinking choke
3. Baggio's 1994 World Cup final miss — the archetypal negative shot

Top 3 Studies:
1. Jordet's 718-kick descriptive corpus (1970–2023; 53% shame behaviour)
2. Baumeister (1984) six experiments + Dutch fNIRS — the overthinking mechanism
3. Jordet's 2009 speeding-up study (37 shootouts/366 kicks; <60% vs. >80%) and the negative-shot analyses

Most Unique Idea: The "negative shot" — a coined category defined by its potential *loss*, turning loss
aversion into a measurable performance variable (63% vs. 89%); and "strategic disengagement," reframing
deliberate not-looking as a control move.

Most Counterintuitive Idea: There are two *opposite* ways to choke (rushing and overthinking), so the cure
for one causes the other; and telling yourself "don't miss" makes a miss more likely.

Biggest Weakness or Open Question: The cognitive mechanisms behind specific misses are "inevitably
speculative" (tiny margins; Jordet flags the Rashford/Rapinoe attributions); the gaze and pause effects
are observational (correlation ≠ causation, crude binary coding); the negative-shot effect replicates in
one study but not another; and no real-time psychophysiological measurement at the spot yet exists.

Best Content Opportunity: "The two ways to choke" (Carragher's rush vs. Rashford's 11-second pause, the
U-shaped pause data) or "the most expensive miss in football" (Baggio 1994 and the negative-shot
statistics).
