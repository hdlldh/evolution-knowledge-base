## 1. Chapter Thesis

The book's final chapter turns to the *manager*: what leadership does in the 2–3 minutes between the final
whistle and the shootout, and why most managers waste them. Built around a forensic comparison of Lionel
Scaloni (Argentina) and Didier Deschamps (France) in the 2022 World Cup final, it argues that great
pressure-leadership is mostly done *in advance*: decide selection and tactics beforehand so the scarce
final minutes can go to *emotional* readiness (Scaloni spent his tending to the distraught Montiel — who
then scored the winner), communicate concisely (across all ten 2022 managers, the teams that finished their
instructions *fastest* won, with no exceptions), speak with **one voice** (every winning manager led the
huddle personally; no losing manager did), and **never ask for volunteers** (under pressure people step
back, not up; volunteering transfers pressure onto the player). It exposes a "behaviour crisis" in
research (almost no study measures what leaders actually *do*), warns against attention-stealing props
(notepads/tablets — used by every losing side, no winning side), notes that managers almost universally
ignore their most important shootout player (the goalkeeper), and closes on the "power of vulnerability"
(Scaloni's tear) and the still-unrealised **psychology revolution** in football: managing a shootout is
managing people, not just players.

## 2. Key Concepts

```
Concept Name: Managing People, Not Just Players (people-first leadership)
Definition: Treating the footballer first as a human being — care, personal contact and emotional support —
as the core of leadership, especially under extreme pressure.
Why It Matters: The chapter's thesis and Scaloni's creed ("the distance between the football player and
people should not be there… he is still a human being"); the manager's most important task is to *dial down*
players' pressure.
How the Author Uses It: Scaloni tending to Montiel; Sjögren's "I'm super proud of you regardless" erasing
Norway's pressure. Argentina's experts call Scaloni "the peaceful leader" (others a "feminine" leader —
"labels rarely applied to football managers"); Di María calls him "our father… very affectionate." His
philosophy accepts loss as the precondition for resilience (after the Saudi upset: "we have to know that we
can lose… if we recover… there will be no stopping us"), the leadership analogue of Ch.4's "missing is natural."
Related Concepts: Leadership under pressure, vulnerability, dial-down-the-pressure, "missing is natural" (Ch.4).
```

```
Concept Name: Pre-Decision (make the hard calls in advance)
Definition: Deciding player selection, order and tactics *before* the game so the final high-pressure minutes
can be spent on emotional readiness, not logistics.
Why It Matters: It is what separates Scaloni (brief, calm, supportive) from Deschamps (2.5 minutes spent
locating and confirming takers). "Plan with the end in mind" — think as hard about who should *end* a match
as who should *start* it.
How the Author Uses It: The Scaloni/Deschamps contrast; the ranked-list-check-who's-present method.
Related Concepts: Concise communication, perception of control, the shortest-time-wins pattern.
```

```
Concept Name: Concise Communication (the shortest-time-wins pattern)
Definition: Under pressure, brief, clear, unambiguous instruction beats long briefings — and it signals preparation.
Why It Matters: Across the ten 2022 World Cup shootout managers, the teams that finished their tactical/
logistical instructions *fastest* won and the slowest lost — no exceptions (the slowest winner, Morocco, was
still faster than the fastest loser, France). Brevity avoids overthinking, projects control, and frees time
for emotional support.
How the Author Uses It: The 10-manager timing analysis; the Netherlands' 40-second flat-structure debate.
Related Concepts: Pre-decision, perception of control, the one-voice principle.
```

```
Concept Name: The One-Voice Principle
Definition: In the final minutes before a shootout, the manager (the highest authority) should do all the
talking; delegation is only for information-gathering, never for the message itself.
Why It Matters: Every winning 2022 manager (Argentina, Croatia, Morocco) led the huddle personally and was
the only voice players heard; every losing manager delegated part of it (assistants, a captain, a keeper
coach) — no exceptions. It's the wrong moment to delegate to staff *or* to players.
How the Author Uses It: The winners-vs-losers pattern; Brazil's Tite abdicating entirely to assistants.
Related Concepts: Top-down management, autocratic-in-crisis, perception of control.
```

```
Concept Name: The Case Against Volunteering
Definition: Publicly asking "who wants to take one?" (or asking players one by one in the huddle) is a poor
selection method that manufactures pressure.
Why It Matters: Volunteering (1) selects those who feel they *should*, not the best takers; (2) transfers
responsibility and pressure onto the player (more pressure → fewer goals); (3) exposes the absence of a plan;
(4) adds cognitive load at the worst moment; (5) can collapse into a fearful, risk-averse atmosphere when
nobody steps up (risk-aversion is contagious).
How the Author Uses It: Japan's fumbling show-of-hands; Hope Powell 2011 ("I had to ask three times… where
are you?"); Spain handing Busquets the paper — vs. the advance, private-conversation alternative.
Related Concepts: Dial-down-the-pressure, advance selection, risk contagion.
```

```
Concept Name: Autocratic in Crisis, Democratic in Peacetime (the trust stock)
Definition: Under acute time pressure, teams trust decisive, authoritative, single-leader direction more
than delegation — but that authority is earned by being democratic and investing in players the rest of the time.
Why It Matters: "Weak leadership in peacetime leads to even weaker leadership in wartime"; the most
authoritative manager in the huddle (Scaloni) is plausibly the most democratic elsewhere ("imposition is
not a thing… the player must believe you").
How the Author Uses It: The firefighting study (autocratic builds trust when urgent, democratic when not);
Scaloni's philosophy; trust built in advance is what you draw on under pressure.
Related Concepts: One-voice principle, people-first leadership, perception of control.
```

```
Concept Name: Perception of Control (order projected downward)
Definition: A manager's calm, crisp, well-oiled communication gives players the reassuring impression of a
robust plan — and that impression itself aids performance, even if the control is partly illusory.
Why It Matters: Extends Ch.2's perceived control to the *leader's* signalling; "just the impression of order
and control from the person in charge can have a positive impact."
How the Author Uses It: Scaloni's smooth operation vs. the Dutch/Brazilian chaos.
Related Concepts: Perceived control (Ch.2), concise communication, one-voice.
```

```
Concept Name: Connection vs. Props (eye contact over notepads)
Definition: Face-to-face connection (eye contact, gestures, touch) beats attention-stealing props (notepads,
tablets, paper) in the final pre-shootout moment.
Why It Matters: Direct eye contact builds faster, more durable, more emotional connection; every losing 2022
side used props, no winning side did. Brazil's players spent almost half their two minutes trying to read a
"magnetic" notebook.
How the Author Uses It: The winners-vs-losers prop pattern; Brazil's magnetic notebook; the goalkeeper-neglect finding.
Related Concepts: Making connections, the one-voice principle, cognitive load.
```

```
Concept Name: The "Behaviour Crisis" in Research (the natural-laboratory case)
Definition: Psychology, leadership and coaching research overwhelmingly measure perceptions/appraisals rather
than what people actually *do* — so we know almost nothing, empirically, about real leadership behaviour.
Why It Matters: It justifies Jordet's observational method (raw multi-angle shootout video) and frames the
whole book's "study behaviour in the wild" stance.
How the Author Uses It: 214 leadership studies (<1 in 5 behavioural; 3% of variables); 610 coaching studies
(<1/5 observational); zero published studies of coach behaviour in shootouts.
Related Concepts: Ecological validity (Ch.0), the natural laboratory, the psychology revolution.
```

```
Concept Name: The Fourth (Psychology) Revolution — still to come
Definition: Football has had tactical, physical and analytics revolutions since 2000; the predicted
psychology revolution (Wenger) has not arrived — management is "stuck where it always has been."
Why It Matters: The book's closing frame — the shootout is a microcosm of a game that treats pressure as a
technical/analytics problem rather than a human one.
How the Author Uses It: Wenger 2010 ("the next area of development") and 2024 ("has there been progress on
performing under pressure? I don't think so").
Related Concepts: The behaviour crisis, people-first leadership, the power of vulnerability.
```

## 3. Key Claims

```
Claim: The manager who communicates instructions fastest wins the shootout.
Type: Empirical (observational)
Evidence Provided: Across all ten 2022 World Cup shootout managers, shortest instruction time → win, longest
→ loss, no exceptions; the slowest winner (Morocco) beat the fastest loser (France).
Strength of Support: Moderate. A striking, exceptionless pattern — but n = 10, observational, and plausibly a
proxy for preparation rather than a cause (Argentina was aided by a prior shootout).
```

```
Claim: Every winning manager led the pre-shootout huddle with one voice; no losing manager did.
Type: Empirical (observational)
Evidence Provided: Argentina/Croatia/Morocco managers spoke personally and solely; France, Japan, Spain,
Netherlands, Brazil all delegated part of it. No exceptions in the 2022 data.
Strength of Support: Moderate (clean pattern, small n = 8 managers; correlational).
```

```
Claim: Asking for volunteers (or asking players one by one publicly) is a harmful selection method.
Type: Interpretive / Prescriptive
Evidence Provided: Five mechanisms (responsibility transfer, no-plan exposure, cognitive load, contagion of
risk-aversion); Japan's fumbling; Powell 2011; up to 30% of players decline the manager's offer.
Strength of Support: Moderate (well-argued, supported by cases and interview data; the "up to 30% decline" stat is cited).
```

```
Claim: Under acute pressure, authoritative single-leader direction is trusted more than delegation — but
authority must be pre-earned democratically.
Type: Empirical / Interpretive
Evidence Provided: A German emergency (firefighting) study — autocratic builds more trust when immediate
action is needed, democratic when not; classic extreme-condition studies; Scaloni as autocratic-in-huddle /
democratic-elsewhere.
Strength of Support: Moderate. Grounded in leadership research, but the classic studies are "quite old" and
the Scaloni read is interpretive.
```

```
Claim: Props (notepads/tablets) in the final moment can cost connection and readiness.
Type: Interpretive (observational)
Evidence Provided: Every losing 2022 side used props, no winning side did; Brazil's players spent ~half their
two minutes reading a "magnetic" notebook; eye-contact neuroscience.
Strength of Support: Weak-to-Moderate. A vivid correlation (n = 5 shootouts); Jordet concedes it "might sound unlikely."
```

```
Claim: Managers almost universally neglect the goalkeeper — their most important shootout player.
Type: Empirical (observational)
Evidence Provided: Only Scaloni personally addressed his keeper pre-shootout; the rest did not (Croatia's
Dalić almost pointedly ignored his), while teammates routinely came to support the keeper.
Strength of Support: Moderate (clean observational finding; small sample).
```

```
Claim: There is a "behaviour crisis" — research ignores what leaders/coaches actually do.
Type: Empirical / Interpretive
Evidence Provided: 214 leadership studies (<1 in 5 measure actual behaviour; 3% of variables behavioural);
610 coaching studies (<1/5 observational); zero on shootout coach behaviour.
Strength of Support: Strong (reviews cited), as a critique of the field.
```

```
Claim: The psychology revolution in football has not yet arrived.
Type: Interpretive
Evidence Provided: Wenger's 2010 prediction vs. his 2024 verdict ("has there been progress on performing
under pressure? I don't think so"); management "remains stuck."
Strength of Support: Moderate (expert testimony + the chapter's observational findings).
```

## 4. Frameworks, Models, and Mental Models

```
Name: Scaloni's Leadership-Under-Pressure Checklist
Description: The key practices for team management in extreme stress, distilled from the 2022 final.
Components: (1) Thorough preparation; (2) as many key decisions as possible made in advance (freeing focus
for emotional readiness); (3) concise, effective communication; (4) receptivity to input; (5) the flexibility
and agility to communicate with whoever needs it most in the moment.
How It Works: Front-load logistics so the scarce final minutes go to people; use assistants as extra eyes/
ears while keeping one voice.
When It Is Useful: Any leader facing a high-stakes, time-boxed, emotionally loaded moment.
Limitations: Distilled from a single (winning) case; hard to separate from talent and luck.
```

```
Name: The Case-Against-Volunteers (5 consequences) + the alternative
Description: Why public volunteering fails, and what to do instead.
Components (failures): wrong selectors; responsibility/pressure transfer; no-plan exposure; cognitive load;
contagious risk-aversion when no one steps up.
Alternative: ask all squad players *in advance and privately*, open-ended (confidence, experience, technique/
routine); build a ranked list; after 120 min, check who's present against it; communicate selection
beforehand; players retain the right to opt out; the manager owns the outcome publicly.
How It Works: Removes the in-the-moment social gamble; keeps pressure off the players.
When It Is Useful: Selecting anyone for a high-stakes, feared task within a team.
Limitations: You can't fully pre-list who'll be on the pitch after 120 minutes (mitigated by "plan the end").
```

```
Name: Autocratic-in-Crisis / Democratic-in-Peacetime (contingency + trust stock)
Description: Match leadership style to urgency, and bank trust in advance.
Components: Urgent → decisive, single-voice, authoritative; non-urgent → democratic, participative; trust
built in calm times is the reserve you spend under pressure.
How It Works: Daily investment in players' opinions earns the right to command crisply when speed matters.
When It Is Useful: Any leader alternating between routine and crisis.
Limitations: "Old" classic evidence; the urgent/non-urgent boundary is fuzzy; risks over-authoritarianism if trust wasn't banked.
```

```
Name: The Four Football Revolutions
Description: A frame for where the game has and hasn't evolved since 2000.
Components: Tactical → physical (sports science) → analytics → (predicted) psychology.
How It Works: The first three arrived; the fourth (performing-under-pressure/people leadership) has not.
When It Is Useful: Positioning the whole book as the case for the missing revolution.
Limitations: A rhetorical frame, not a measured claim.
```

## 5. Research and Evidence

```
Study / Research: The shortest-time-wins timing analysis (2022 World Cup)
Researchers: Geir Jordet (raw multi-angle shootout video)
Year: 2022.
Research Question: Does how long a manager takes to deliver pre-shootout instructions relate to the outcome?
Method: Timed the tactical/logistical instruction phase for all ten managers across the five 2022 shootouts.
Key Finding: Shortest time → win, longest → loss, no exceptions; slowest winner (Morocco) faster than fastest
loser (France); Argentina fastest (aided by a prior shootout), Croatia 20s+ faster the second time.
How the Author Uses It: The headline behavioural signal of leadership preparation.
Important Limitations: n = 10, observational; likely a proxy for preparation, not a direct cause.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: None identified.
```

```
Study / Research: The "behaviour crisis" reviews
Researchers: Not specified (three reviews).
Year: Not specified.
Research Question: Does research measure actual leadership/coaching behaviour?
Method: Reviews of 214 leadership-behaviour studies and 610 sports-coaching studies.
Key Finding: <1 in 5 leadership studies measure actual behaviour (only 3% of variables are behavioural);
<1/5 coaching studies use behavioural observation; zero published studies on coach behaviour in shootouts.
How the Author Uses It: To justify observational, in-the-wild study of leadership.
Important Limitations: A field critique; his own observational method has small samples and no controls.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: None identified.
```

```
Study / Research: Autocratic vs. democratic leadership under urgency (firefighting)
Researchers: Not specified (recent German study); plus older classic studies.
Year: Recent (German) + classic (old).
Research Question: Which leadership style builds trust under acute pressure?
Method: Study of severely pressured emergency contexts (e.g. firefighting).
Key Finding: When immediate action is required, autocratic leadership creates more trust than democratic;
in less urgent settings, democratic creates more trust.
How the Author Uses It: The evidence base for one-voice/top-down at the moment of the shootout.
Important Limitations: Cross-domain; classic studies "quite old"; perceptions of leadership may have shifted.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: Notes the classic studies' age.
```

```
Study / Research: Eye contact and connection
Researchers: Not specified (neuropsychological research).
Year: Not specified.
Research Question: What does direct eye contact do for communication?
Method: Neuropsychological research.
Key Finding: Direct eye contact facilitates faster, more durable and more emotional connection.
How the Author Uses It: To argue against attention-stealing props in the huddle.
Important Limitations: General finding applied by analogy.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: None identified.
```

```
Study / Research: Players declining the manager's penalty offer
Researchers: Geir Jordet (interviews)
Year: Not specified.
Research Question: How often do players say no when asked to take a shootout penalty?
Method: Interviews with players about penalty shootouts.
Key Finding: Up to 30% turn down the manager's offer.
How the Author Uses It: To show why asking one-by-one publicly is risky (refusals spread doubt).
Important Limitations: Self-report; "up to 30%" is an upper bound.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: None identified.
```

```
Study / Research: Contagion of risk-taking / risk-aversion
Researchers: Not specified.
Year: Not specified.
Research Question: Is willingness to take risks contagious?
Method: Cited evidence from other contexts.
Key Finding: Willingness to take risks is contagious; by extension, seeing teammates decline the penalty
risk likely makes others more risk-averse and anxious.
How the Author Uses It: Consequence #5 of the volunteer approach.
Important Limitations: Extrapolated to penalties by analogy.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: None identified.
```

## 7. Cases and Stories

```
Case Title: Scaloni and Montiel — leadership as emotional triage (2022 World Cup final)
People / Organization: Lionel Scaloni & Gonzalo Montiel (Argentina) vs. France
Context: Argentina were 3-2 up with three minutes left when Montiel's handball gave France the 3-3
equaliser; a shootout for the World Cup loomed.
What Happened: Scaloni made his two team talks extraordinarily short (<10s, then 15s), and spent the bulk of
his time on the devastated Montiel — a shoulder-bump, then a return with a whisper and a tap on the chest,
twice asking if he was up for a penalty (the second time, yes). His assistants fed him whispered guidance
("this player needs support"); the players heard only his voice.
Outcome: Argentina scored all four; Montiel scored the winner — Argentina's first World Cup since 1986.
Concept Illustrated: People-first leadership; pre-decision freeing time for emotional readiness; one voice.
Why This Case Is Useful: The chapter's set-piece; a masterclass in leadership as emotional triage.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Deschamps's slow taker-hunt — the contrast
People / Organization: Didier Deschamps & Guy Stéphan (France)
Context: The same final, the same two minutes — but France had not had a prior shootout in the tournament.
What Happened: Deschamps spent ~2.5 minutes locating and confirming takers — a 360° pirouette scanning the
group before confirming Tchouaméni; a hesitant seven-second walk with seven visual scans to reach Kolo Muani
— a "seemingly improvised, impersonal" process, shadowed throughout by Stéphan and his notepad.
Outcome: Coman and the much-deliberated Tchouaméni missed; France lost.
Concept Illustrated: The cost of unmade decisions; time drained by logistics instead of support.
Why This Case Is Useful: The direct foil to Scaloni; shows what *not* to do with the final minutes.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: The shortest-time-wins pattern (10 managers, no exceptions)
People / Organization: The ten managers of the 2022 World Cup shootouts
Context: Jordet timed each manager's instruction phase from raw multi-angle video.
What Happened: The teams that finished instructions fastest won; the slowest lost — with no exceptions, and a
clear gap (Morocco, slowest winner, still faster than France, fastest loser). Argentina were fastest; Croatia
were 20s+ faster their second time.
Outcome: A clean behavioural signal linking brevity/preparation to winning.
Concept Illustrated: Concise communication; pre-decision; measurable leadership behaviour.
Why This Case Is Useful: A rare quantified leadership finding; memorable and testable.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: The Netherlands' 40-second debate (flat structure under pressure)
People / Organization: Louis van Gaal, Frans Hoek, Danny Blind; Frenkie de Jong, Davy Klaassen (Netherlands)
Context: A thorough plan (Hoek read 11 names off a note) undone by process.
What Happened: An intense multi-voice discussion (players and coaches objecting/disagreeing) ran ~40 seconds;
the stereotypically flat Dutch style (everyone has a voice) slowed selection while Argentina, done quickly
next to them, may have induced competitive panic.
Outcome: The Netherlands lost; van Gaal missed the chance to give individual support.
Concept Illustrated: The cost of delegation/consensus at the moment of crisis; one-voice violated.
Why This Case Is Useful: A vivid failure of "everyone has a say" under acute time pressure.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: Don't ask for volunteers — Japan, Spain and Hope Powell
People / Organization: Hajime Moriyasu (Japan); Luis Enrique & Busquets (Spain); Hope Powell (England women, 2011)
Context: Three teams that made selection a public, in-the-moment ask.
What Happened: Japan's show-of-hands stalled (a sub even nominated a teammate); Minamino, Mitoma, Yoshida
missed. Spain's Enrique picked three then handed Busquets the paper to find the rest — all three chosen
missed. Powell "had to ask three times before a single player put their hand up" and later concluded "Don't
ask for volunteers… at a moment of pressure, they usually step back."
Outcome: All three lost; Moriyasu and Powell both vowed to select differently.
Concept Illustrated: The case against volunteering; under pressure people step back.
Why This Case Is Useful: Three converging failures + two managers' explicit recantations.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: "I'm super proud of you regardless" — dialling the pressure to zero
People / Organization: Martin Sjögren & Caroline Graham Hansen (Norway women, 2019 World Cup vs. Australia)
Context: Right before a round-of-16 shootout.
What Happened: Sjögren told the players, "It doesn't matter what you do, I am super proud of you regardless."
Graham Hansen: "just like that, all the pressure was gone."
Outcome: Norway scored all their kicks and went through.
Concept Illustrated: The manager's core task — dial down the pressure — achieved in one sentence.
Why This Case Is Useful: A pocket-sized, imitable leadership intervention.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Tite's ravine trauma and total withdrawal (Brazil)
People / Organization: Tite & assistant Cléber Xavier (Brazil), 2022 World Cup vs. Croatia
Context: Tite removed himself entirely from the shootout, pacing 20 m away, looking paralysed.
What Happened: In 1986 Tite's team lost a final on penalties; unable to sleep, he crashed his car into a
ravine that night ("we could have lost our lives… I got traumatised, big time"). Since then "Tite does not
deal with penalties" — Xavier runs them, with a notebook, in the least-structured briefing of any 2022 team.
Outcome: Brazil's players were visibly unfocused (Neymar, Marquinhos, Antony disengaging); Brazil lost.
Concept Illustrated: A leader's abdication (and its human cause); the one-voice principle violated at the extreme.
Why This Case Is Useful: A poignant, human explanation for a leadership failure.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: Brazil's magnetic notebook (props kill connection)
People / Organization: Cléber Xavier & the Brazil players vs. Croatia
Context: ~2 minutes on the sideline before the shootout.
What Happened: Xavier's notebook became "magnetic" — Thiago Silva stared at it for over a minute, and the
players spent almost half their available time trying to read names/numbers on paper instead of connecting.
Outcome: A minute of shared attention consumed by a prop; no winning 2022 side used props, every losing side did.
Concept Illustrated: Connection vs. props; eye contact over notepads.
Why This Case Is Useful: The concrete image behind the props argument.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: Scaloni's tear — the power of vulnerability
People / Organization: Lionel Scaloni (Argentina)
Context: The instant Montiel's kick wins the World Cup.
What Happened: No bravado, no fist-pump — a focused, calm face, then the slightest smile and a small tear
forming in the corner of his eye.
Outcome: "Few images in football more expressive of the power of vulnerability."
Concept Illustrated: People-first leadership; vulnerability as strength; the anti-macho leader.
Why This Case Is Useful: The book's emotional coda; a counter-image to "toughness" leadership.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

## 9. Counterintuitive Insights

```
Insight: The manager who talks least wins.
Common Belief: A big, detailed, motivational team talk is what a leader owes the team before the biggest moment.
Author's Argument: Brevity signals preparation, avoids overthinking, projects control and frees time for
emotional support; the fastest managers won every 2022 shootout.
Evidence: The 10-manager timing pattern (no exceptions).
Why It Is Surprising: It inverts the "inspiring speech" model of big-moment leadership.
```

```
Insight: Never ask for volunteers — under pressure, people step back.
Common Belief: Asking for volunteers ensures you get willing, committed takers.
Author's Argument: It selects the dutiful over the able, transfers pressure onto the raised hand, exposes the
lack of a plan, and can spread risk-aversion; decide in advance and ask privately.
Evidence: Powell 2011; Japan; up to 30% decline; risk-aversion contagion.
Why It Is Surprising: The "fair, democratic" method is the pressure-maximising one.
```

```
Insight: The most authoritative leader in a crisis is the most democratic the rest of the time.
Common Belief: Leaders are either autocratic or democratic.
Author's Argument: Style should be contingent on urgency, and crisis authority is *earned* by peacetime
democracy and trust — Scaloni embodies both.
Evidence: The firefighting study; Scaloni's philosophy and behaviour.
Why It Is Surprising: It dissolves the autocratic/democratic binary into a sequence.
```

```
Insight: A notepad can help cost you a World Cup.
Common Belief: A coach's notes are harmless, even diligent, preparation.
Author's Argument: Props steal eye contact and attention at the exact moment connection matters most; every
losing 2022 side used them, no winning side did.
Evidence: Brazil's magnetic notebook; the winners-vs-losers prop pattern; eye-contact neuroscience.
Why It Is Surprising: A tool of "preparation" becomes a liability in the moment.
```

```
Insight: Managers ignore their single most important shootout player — the goalkeeper.
Common Belief: The keeper, with the specialist coach, needs no managerial attention.
Author's Argument: The keeper is the only player involved in every kick, yet only one 2022 manager addressed
his keeper (some pointedly ignored theirs) — while teammates instinctively supported him.
Evidence: The 2022 observational analysis.
Why It Is Surprising: The most pivotal player is the most neglected by leadership.
```

```
Insight: Elite football has perfected physical support and neglected psychological support.
Common Belief: Top teams, with all their resources, must support players comprehensively.
```

## 11. Tensions, Contradictions, and Open Questions

```
Issue: Shortest-time-wins — cause, or proxy for preparation (and confounded)?
Author's Position: Frames brevity as beneficial (clarity, control, freed time) and a signal of preparation.
Possible Counterargument: n = 10, observational; fast time likely *reflects* preparation rather than causing
the win; Argentina's speed was aided by a prior shootout in the tournament.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Controlled or larger-sample study separating communication time from underlying preparation.
```

```
Issue: One-voice / autocratic-in-crisis vs. the book's collective-ownership ethos (Ch.2/Ch.4).
Author's Position: Delegate for information, not for the message; the huddle needs one authoritative voice.
Possible Counterargument: This sits in tension with giving players ownership/process (Ch.2) and the flat,
belonging-based team model (Ch.4); the boundary between "empowering" and "commanding" is context-dependent.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: When does authoritative single-voice help vs. when does shared ownership
help, controlling for pre-built trust?
```

```
Issue: The props claim.
Author's Position: Props cost connection and readiness (winners used none, losers all did).
Possible Counterargument: n = 5 shootouts; the prop may be a marker of poor preparation rather than a cause;
Jordet concedes it "might sound unlikely."
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Whether prop use predicts outcome controlling for preparation quality.
```

```
Issue: Predictability (announce takers in advance) vs. players dreading their kick.
Author's Position: The benefits of predictability outweigh the risk of pre-game dread.
Possible Counterargument: Naming a taker days ahead could increase anticipatory anxiety for some players.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Comparing pre-announced vs. in-the-moment selection on conversion and reported anxiety.
```

```
Issue: The observational method's own limits.
Author's Position: The "behaviour crisis" justifies studying real behaviour from video.
Possible Counterargument: His own analyses are small-n, uncontrolled and correlational — the same critique in
reverse (rich behavioural description, weak causal inference).
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Larger, coded, hypothesis-testing behavioural datasets across many tournaments.
```

## 13. Psychology Connections

- **Leadership under pressure / extreme-context leadership**: crisis events, "this can't happen to us," pre-event trust.
- **Social contagion**: risk-taking (and risk-aversion) spreads through a group — the volunteer-collapse mechanism.
- **Responsibility/pressure transfer**: raising a hand shifts accountability and load onto the player (more pressure → fewer goals).
- **Perceived control (Ch.2 callback)**: a leader's projected order/plan raises players' sense of control — even if partly illusory.
- **Cognitive load & overthinking (Ch.1 callback)**: long briefings and prop-reading add load at the worst moment.
- **Nonverbal connection**: eye contact and touch for faster, deeper, more durable connection.
- **Vulnerability & authentic leadership**: Scaloni's tear; "the player must believe you."
- **The ecological-validity/behaviour-crisis critique (Ch.0 callback)**: study what people *do*, not what they say they'd do.

## 14. Mathematics and Decision Science Connections

- **A clean ordinal signal**: shortest-instruction-time perfectly rank-separated winners from losers (n = 10) — striking, with the usual correlation-vs-causation caveat.
- **Base rates**: 20–30% of knockout games go to shootouts; from 2026, finalists play five straight knockout games — the odds of a shootout are high, so not preparing "flies in the face of the odds."
- **Backward planning / "plan the end"**: selecting for the 120-minute-plus lineup you can't fully predict — decision under uncertainty with a ranked list + last-second check.
- **Contingent policy**: autocratic vs. democratic conditioned on urgency (a switch, not a fixed style).
- **Decline rate**: up to 30% of players say no — a base rate that makes public one-by-one asking risky.
- **Construct validity / measurement**: the "behaviour crisis" is a measurement-error argument (proxies vs. the real variable).

## 15. Sports Connections

**Direct examples from the book:** managerial behaviour across the 2022 World Cup shootouts (Argentina,
France, Netherlands, Japan, Spain, Brazil, Croatia, Morocco) plus England women (Powell 2011) and Norway
women (Sjögren 2019); explicit cross-domain transfer — **extreme-context leadership** (military, medical, law
enforcement, firefighting, crisis response).

**Inferred applications (mine):**
- **Any team endgame/timeout leadership**: basketball final-possession huddles, cricket super-overs — the
  pre-decision, one-voice, brevity and support principles transfer.
- **Selection for feared roles**: choosing a closer, a clutch shooter, a lead presenter — never by public volunteering.
- **Officiating/staff role design**: information-gathering delegation vs. message delegation.
- **Coach education**: timing and prop audits as concrete, teachable behavioural interventions.

## 16. AI and Machine Learning Connections

*(All inferred unless noted.)*
- **Evaluation validity (strong bridge)**: the "behaviour crisis" is exactly the eval problem in ML — measuring
  proxies/appraisals instead of real behaviour/outcomes; rich behavioural data (the video analysis) is the
  "in-the-wild eval" analogue.
- **Context-conditional policy / mode-switching**: autocratic-in-crisis / democratic-in-peacetime is a
  policy that switches on a latency/urgency signal — decisive low-latency action vs. deliberative slow thinking.
- **Latency as a readiness proxy**: the shortest-time-wins pattern parallels using response time as a signal
  of a system's preparedness/confidence.
- **Attention/channel management**: props stealing eye contact ≈ competing attention sinks degrading a channel;
  "one voice" ≈ a single coherent signal vs. conflicting inputs.
- **Trust/warm start**: "bank trust in peacetime" ≈ pre-training/warm-starting a system so it performs when the load spikes.
- **Influence propagation**: risk-aversion contagion ≈ cascade/influence dynamics in multi-agent populations.
