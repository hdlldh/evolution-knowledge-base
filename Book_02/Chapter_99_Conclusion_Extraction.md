## 1. Chapter Thesis

The Epilogue is the book's synthesis and forward argument. Football, a multi-billion-pound industry, still
treats penalty preparation with avoidance — chaotic, short-term, "one game at a time" — so players selected
to take the most consequential kick of their lives are "sent into the lion's den unprepared and unprotected."
The fault is not individual coaches or players but a traditionalist *culture* that assumed you either have
the courage or you don't. Jordet traces how the "lottery" belief solidified (the biggest superstars missing
on the biggest stages; FIFA's own Blatter-era attempts to abolish the shootout via the golden goal), then
delivers the book's most important epistemic caveat: the contrarian "penalties are *not* a lottery" consensus
is a **mixed blessing**, because chance genuinely plays a larger role in a penalty than in a whole game — and
data findings date fast (the famous "first-mover advantage" decayed from 61% to 49% as humans adapted). The
resolution is to hold *both* — skill/preparation *and* the chance element — and to build the coming
**psychology revolution**: not to stop players missing, but to help them accept being vulnerable and cope.
It closes on the universal human core: over the ball, alone, the taker is "first and foremost a human being"
— "their pressure is our pressure."

## 2. Key Concepts

```
Concept Name: Football's Culture of Avoidance
Definition: The sport's systemic failure to prioritise, structure or prepare for penalties — postponing them
as "everything else" behind winning the next match, which Jordet reads as fundamental avoidance.
Why It Matters: It is the root cause the book diagnoses; a multi-billion-pound industry leaves a
match-deciding event "oddly unstructured, improvised and reactive."
How the Author Uses It: The four opening consultancy anecdotes (the night-before-the-quarter-final email; the
ambush group presentation; "show us what you got!"; the coach who spent 28 of 30 minutes on his own philosophy).
In the early decades this avoidance took the form of a "just get it over with" escapism — the collective
version of Ch.1's *speeding up* (treating the whistle as a starting gun to end the dread).
Related Concepts: The problem is the culture, the lottery belief, speeding up (Ch.1), the psychology revolution.
```

```
Concept Name: The Problem Is the Culture, Not the Individuals
Definition: Coaches who fail players in shootouts (Deschamps, Enrique, Moriyasu) were themselves never taught
what to do — the deficit is cultural and generational, not personal.
Why It Matters: It reframes blame: "the problem is not individual coaches, any more than it is individual
players. The problem is the culture" — traditionalist, conservative, assuming courage is innate. "We need to
break this cycle."
How the Author Uses It: To de-personalise the critique and locate the fix at the level of norms and education.
Related Concepts: Culture of avoidance, confidence-as-innate myth, the psychology revolution.
```

```
Concept Name: The Mixed Blessing of "Not a Lottery" (hold skill AND chance)
Definition: The now-consensus rejection of the lottery metaphor rightly restores the value of skill and
preparation — but it wrongly neglects the equally correct fact that parts of a penalty are genuinely beyond
the player's control.
Why It Matters: The book's central epistemic resolution: chance plays a *larger* role in a penalty than in a
whole game; unless you are among the very best, you can do everything right and still miss. Preparation must
acknowledge the chance element, not deny it.
How the Author Uses It: To correct the "self-proclaimed football intellectuals" who unconditionally reject
uncontrollability; and to distinguish the taker's mindset (100% self-belief, reject chance) from the
preparer's stance (acknowledge chance).
Related Concepts: The lottery metaphor's protective function, perceived control (Ch.2), skill-vs-chance.
```

```
Concept Name: The Lottery Metaphor's Protective Function
Definition: A coach who calls a lost shootout "a lottery" is, at least, removing responsibility from the
players who missed — a defensive act on their behalf.
Why It Matters: It nuances the book's own critique of the lottery metaphor (Ch.2): the metaphor perpetuates
the "uncontrollable" perception, but it also protects players when the coach failed to protect them beforehand.
How the Author Uses It: A generous re-reading of coaches' post-loss "it's a lottery" quotes.
Related Concepts: The mixed blessing, dial-down-the-pressure (Ch.6), taking responsibility.
```

```
Concept Name: Findings Date Fast (the adaptation/epistemic-humility principle)
Definition: Human behaviour is never as simple as statistical output; people adopt and adapt (faster now than
before), so pivotal findings can become unobservable — data must be constantly updated and conclusions revised.
Why It Matters: The book's meta-caveat about its own claims ("my research is no exception"); it invites
replication and expects some findings to survive and others not.
How the Author Uses It: The first-mover-advantage decay (61%→49%) as the flagship demonstration; his own
bias-awareness (his behaviour-analysis fascination could over-attribute meaning to arbitrary behaviour).
Related Concepts: First-mover advantage, bias awareness, numbers-without-context.
```

```
Concept Name: The Coming Psychology Revolution (accept vulnerability, don't eliminate misses)
Definition: Extending psychology's "hesitant entry" into the shootout — control strategies, teamwork,
taker-protectors, pressure training and sensitive communication — with the aim not of removing pressure or
stopping misses, but of helping players accept vulnerability and cope.
Why It Matters: The book's forward argument; the new generation of players and progressive coaches (with
unprecedented access to footage/resources and comfort with vulnerability — Haaland admitting his nerves) are
equipped to raise penalties the way set-pieces were raised.
How the Author Uses It: The optimistic close; psychology as "an entire cognitive, emotional and social event."
Related Concepts: The set-piece analogy, vulnerability (Ch.6), the collective toolkit (Ch.4).
```

```
Concept Name: "Their Pressure Is Our Pressure" (the human universality)
Definition: Over the ball, alone, the taker is seen first and foremost as a human being under pressure —
subject to the full range of human emotion — which is why the shootout speaks to us so profoundly.
Why It Matters: The book's final thesis and the reason the penalty shootout is the ideal "natural laboratory"
for pressure that transfers to everyone's life.
How the Author Uses It: The closing lines; ties the whole book back to the reader.
Related Concepts: The natural laboratory (Ch.0), vulnerability, managing people not players (Ch.6).
```

## 4. Frameworks, Models, and Mental Models

```
Name: The Four Eras of the Penalty Shootout
Description: A historical arc of how football has related to the shootout.
Components: (1) Raw escapism / "it's a lottery" (1970s–2000s, avoidance, FIFA trying to abolish it);
(2) the anti-lottery skill/science era (1998–, "not a lottery," training helps); (3) the data/analytics
revolution (numbers on shots, positioning, game theory); (4) the coming psychology revolution (accepting
vulnerability, coping, communication).
How It Works: Each era corrected the last but carried its own blind spot (escapism denied control; the
anti-lottery era denied chance; analytics denied context).
When It Is Useful: Positioning the whole book as the case for era four while balancing eras two and three.
Limitations: A narrative synthesis, not a measured periodisation.
```

```
Name: Skill-AND-Chance (the both/and resolution)
Description: Hold two truths at once — penalties are trainable (skill/preparation matter) *and* partly
uncontrollable (chance is real, larger than in a whole game).
Components: For the taker over the ball: 100% self-belief, reject chance. For the preparer/coach: acknowledge
the chance element and the low-control perception, and prepare the psychology accordingly.
How It Works: Different stances for different roles/moments resolve the apparent contradiction.
When It Is Useful: Preparing for any high-variance, high-stakes task where both effort and luck matter.
Limitations: Requires holding a productive contradiction; easy to collapse into one side.
```

```
Name: Bias-Aware Method (acknowledge and act with your biases)
Description: Recognise your own biases and reason with them in mind, rather than pretending to none.
Components: Name the bias (e.g. Jordet's fascination with behaviour analysis → risk of reading meaning into
arbitrary behaviour) → discount for it → invite replication/challenge.
How It Works: Treats one's own conclusions as provisional and updatable.
When It Is Useful: Any observational, interpretation-heavy analysis (including this book's).
Limitations: Self-declared bias-awareness doesn't eliminate the bias; still small-n and observational.
```

## 5. Research and Evidence

```
Study / Research: First-mover advantage in shootouts (and its decay)
Researchers: Ignacio Palacios-Huerta (Spanish economist), 2010; plus successive larger studies
Year: 2010 → later, updated samples.
Research Question: Does the team shooting first in a shootout win more often?
Method: Archival analysis of shootout outcomes by shooting order; replicated on larger/updated datasets.
Key Finding: 61% first-shooter wins (2010) → 55% → 51% → 49% (largest, most recent) — the advantage decayed to
nothing; captains almost universally chose to go first after 2010, and an ABBA format change was proposed then shelved.
How the Author Uses It: The flagship case that findings date fast and humans adapt; a caution on purely
data-driven analysis.
Important Limitations: The mechanism (how teams neutralised it) is inferred; illustrates adaptation, not a fixed law.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: Yes — the successive replications are the whole point (the effect shrank across them).
```

```
Study / Research: Golden-goal history (FIFA's attempt to replace the shootout)
Researchers: Not applicable (rule history).
Year: Introduced 1993; used at men's Euros 1996/2000, men's WC 1998/2002; 2003 Women's WC final.
Research Question: Can extra-time "sudden death" scoring replace the shootout?
Method: Rule trial across major tournaments.
Key Finding: The golden goal paralysed extra-time play and induced extreme caution rather than opening the
game; abandoned "with no particular complaints from fans."
How the Author Uses It: Evidence of the sport's decades-long denial/avoidance of the shootout (Blatter era).
Important Limitations: Descriptive history.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: None identified.
```

```
Study / Research: Team-level shootout performance patterns and the early literature
Researchers: Various scientists/analysts/authors (turn of the century)
Year: c. 1998–2005 (six penalty books cited).
Research Question: Are penalties a lottery, and can they be trained?
Method: Early studies (mainly goalkeeper anticipation; shot placement left/right/middle, power vs. precision).
Key Finding: Consensus that penalties are NOT a lottery and that training benefits takers and keepers;
observable team patterns (Germany win; England/Netherlands/Spain tend to lose).
How the Author Uses It: To situate his own work and to flag the consensus's blind spot (denying chance).
Important Limitations: Early-era focus on the kick/keeper, not the psychology/context.
Replication or Controversy Mentioned: The book itself challenges the "unconditional" anti-lottery stance.
```

```
Case Title: The first-mover advantage that vanished
People / Organization: Ignacio Palacios-Huerta (economist) and the shootout-order literature
Context: A landmark 2010 finding that shaped coin-toss behaviour and nearly changed the rules.
What Happened: 61% of first-shooting teams won (2010) → captains rushed to go first → an ABBA format was
proposed → but larger, updated samples found 55%, 51%, then 49%: the edge decayed to nothing as teams adapted,
and the format change was shelved.
Outcome: A famous, adopted, rule-influencing finding that dated fast.
Concept Illustrated: Findings date fast; humans adapt; effect-size decay; the peril of purely data-driven analysis.
Why This Case Is Useful: A crisp, transferable epistemics lesson (and the book's resolution of the game-theory tradition).
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Blatter's crusade and the golden goal
People / Organization: Sepp Blatter (FIFA gen. sec. 1981–98, president 1998–2015); FIFA
Context: Football's governing body treating the shootout as the wrong, temporary tie-breaker.
What Happened: Blatter ("penalties… it is a tragedy") campaigned to replace the shootout; FIFA's 1993 "golden
goal" (used at Euros 1996/2000, WC 1998/2002, the 2003 Women's WC final) paralysed extra time and was quietly
abandoned. When Blatter argued "penalties are not about a team, they are about individuals," Jordet directly
rebuts him — "I beg to differ: a penalty kick is a team event" — restating the book's Ch.4 thesis against FIFA's own president.
Outcome: The shootout survived, but the governing body's disdain helped entrench teams' avoidance.
Concept Illustrated: The sport's institutional denial/avoidance of the shootout.
Why This Case Is Useful: Explains *why* the lottery/avoidance culture took hold from the top down.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: The superstar-miss roll call
People / Organization: Platini & Zico (1986 WC), Maradona (1990 WC), van Basten (1992 Euros), Baresi &
Baggio (1994 WC), Maldini & (almost) the whole Dutch team (2000 Euros)
Context: Why the "lottery" belief solidified.
What Happened: The very best players missed on the biggest stages, spectacularly and with devastating
consequences, so "the lesson to everyone was obvious: nobody could control a shootout. It was a lottery."
Outcome: Superstar failure → the entrenched belief that skill can't save you and the shootout is uncontrollable.
Concept Illustrated: How the lottery belief was learned; skill alone doesn't guarantee the penalty.
Why This Case Is Useful: A memorable evidentiary basis for the "lottery" mindset the book overturns.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

## 13. Psychology Connections

- **Avoidance at the cultural level**: an entire sport avoiding a feared, high-stakes task — the collective form of the individual avoidance of Ch.1.
- **Learned helplessness / the lottery belief**: superstar failure taught everyone the outcome was uncontrollable.
- **Acceptance & vulnerability**: the forward argument — accept exposure, then cope (Ch.1–2, Ch.6 callbacks).
- **Bias & reflexivity**: acknowledging one's own biases (the behaviour-analysis fascination) and acting with them in mind.
- **Empathy / universality**: "their pressure is our pressure" — the shootout as a mirror for the human experience of pressure.
- **Defensive attribution (protective function)**: the lottery metaphor as a shield removing responsibility from the misser.

## 14. Mathematics and Decision Science Connections

- **Effect-size decay / regression / adaptation**: the first-mover advantage falling 61%→55%→51%→49% across successive, larger samples — a clean case of a discovered edge eroding.
- **Game theory (Palacios-Huerta) & minimax**: the shooting-order advantage and the ABBA-vs-AB format debate; the decay arguably vindicates convergence to un-exploitable play.
- **Skill vs. chance / variance decomposition**: chance plays a larger role for a single penalty than for a whole match; "do everything right and still miss."
- **Non-stationarity**: "old observations become dated very fast" — the environment (opponents) adapts, so yesterday's model degrades.
- **Data-without-context / Goodhart-adjacent**: numbers without psychological understanding mislead; optimising a measured edge invites adaptation that erases it.

## 15. Sports Connections

**Direct examples from the book:** shootout culture, rule history (golden goal), and the first-mover-advantage
literature across football; the set-piece analogy (corners, free kicks, throw-ins raised by specialist
coaches) as the template for how penalties could be professionalised.

**Inferred applications (mine):**
- **Any rare, high-stakes, feared team event**: the "we avoid preparing for it" pathology transfers to
  crisis drills, disaster recovery, and "who closes the deal" moments.
- **Coach/analyst education**: the "acknowledge chance + context, not just numbers" stance as a coaching-science principle.
- **Specialist-role professionalisation**: designated protectors and penalty specialists mirror set-piece coaches.

## 16. AI and Machine Learning Connections

*(All inferred unless noted.)*
- **Concept drift / non-stationarity (strong bridge)**: the first-mover-advantage decay is a textbook case —
  a learned pattern degrades as the environment (opponents) adapts; models need continual updating, exactly
  as Jordet says of his findings ("my research is no exception").
- **Adversarial adaptation / Goodhart's law**: once an edge is discovered and exploited, opponents adapt to
  neutralise it — optimising a measured metric erodes it.
- **Numbers without context / interpretability**: "data without contextual and psychological understanding"
  is the interpretability/validity gap — good predictions still need the right features and framing.
- **Bias disclosure / model cards**: the author naming his own biases parallels documenting a model's known biases and limits.
- **Skill vs. noise / irreducible error**: the acknowledged chance element is the irreducible (Bayes) error a
  model can't remove — deny it and you overfit to signal that isn't there.
