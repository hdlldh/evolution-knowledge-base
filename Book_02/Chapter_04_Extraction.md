## 1. Chapter Thesis

Where Chapter 2 was the individual taking control and Chapter 3 was the opponent exploiting pressure, Chapter
4 is the *team*: the penalty is no longer a lone individual's task but a collective one, and how a group
responds together — especially to a miss — often decides the shootout. The chapter's emotional core is "the
longest walk" (back to the centre circle after missing) and the finding that **belonging** is the antidote:
teams that break formation to re-enfold a teammate who missed send a signal that dials down pressure for
everyone still to kick. Its intellectual core is imported from **high-reliability organisations** (hospitals,
aviation): elite performers don't make fewer errors, they *respond* to errors better — so preparing players
means normalising the miss ("missing penalties is natural") rather than chasing positive feelings. The
chapter then catalogues the modern "collective penalty" toolkit — protecting the spot, protecting the taker,
the decoy taker, contagious celebration, the goalkeeper handoff, and a more dominant centre-circle position —
and closes with the "give and take" of penalties (Haaland gifting kicks), framed through Adam Grant's
giver/taker/matcher model. Throughout, Jordet stresses there are *no guarantees*: these are probabilistic,
tailorable tools, and several backfire when done crudely.

## 2. Key Concepts

```
Concept Name: The Longest Walk / Belonging After a Miss
Definition: The walk back to the centre circle after missing — "the loneliest experience in sport" — and the
need to be re-included by teammates at that moment.
Why It Matters: Exclusion fires the same neural/physiological alarm as physical harm; a genuine signal of
belonging at the point of failure can measurably lower pressure for the *later* kickers too.
How the Author Uses It: Rashford (England 2020, no one moved) vs. Italy (Chiellini + Florenzi broke formation
to meet Belotti); the "we ALL walk over" plan that the Dutch U21s rated their most valuable preparation.
Related Concepts: Social support, the shoulder-to-shoulder trap, "missing is natural."
```

```
Concept Name: Recovering From a Miss (the response beats the error)
Definition: A miss is usually a temporary setback; what decides outcomes is the collective *response* to it.
Why It Matters: 49 of 93 shootouts (52.7%, to 2022) were won by a team that missed one or more penalties —
so miss-recovery is not a footnote, it is more than half the story.
How the Author Uses It: The statistical spine of the chapter; motivates normalising misses and building
pickup routines.
Related Concepts: High-reliability organisations, belonging, "missing is natural."
```

```
Concept Name: High-Reliability Organisations (HROs) — respond, don't just avoid
Definition: Domains where failure is likely and costly (hospitals, aviation, firefighting, nuclear plants,
aircraft carriers, oil platforms) that manage errors by *responding* to them mindfully, flexibly and
non-punitively — treating the response as more important than the original error.
Why It Matters: HRO workers make as many mistakes as anyone but don't let mistakes incapacitate them; the
model reframes penalty prep from error-avoidance to error-response.
How the Author Uses It: Borrowed from Groningen medical colleagues; the intellectual pivot from Jordet's
naive 2005 "think positive" approach to his 2007 "missing is natural" approach.
Related Concepts: "Missing is natural," recovering from a miss, error-management culture.
```

```
Concept Name: "Missing Penalties Is Natural" (normalise failure, don't chase good feelings)
Definition: Performing under pressure is "doing what is right and smart regardless of how one feels, and
particularly when one feels bad" — not feeling good or thinking positive.
Why It Matters: Jordet's 2005 positive-thinking approach (only scoring videos, only optimism) was "naive";
the aim of feeling good "misses the mark." Naming and normalising the miss defuses it.
How the Author Uses It: The 2007 U21 presentation ("some of you will miss… the best players miss more");
the HRO logic applied.
Related Concepts: HRO, threat-vs-challenge (Ch.1), acceptance mindset (Ch.2).
```

```
Concept Name: The Collective Penalty (the revolution)
Definition: A penalty — in-game or shootout — is now a team task in which several players have functional
roles to maximise the taker's readiness, not an isolated individual event.
Why It Matters: It rewrites the "leave it to the taker" default; the chapter's organising claim, instantiated
by six concrete sub-strategies.
How the Author Uses It: The umbrella for spot-protection, taker-protection, decoy, celebration, keeper
handoff, and centre-circle strategy.
Related Concepts: All six sub-strategies below; give-and-take.
```

```
Concept Name: The Shoulder-to-Shoulder Formation Trap
Definition: The tight interlocked halfway-line formation (universal since ~2010) signals unity and comforts
those in it — but can *prevent* players from breaking free to support a teammate who missed.
Why It Matters: A "unity" ritual can backfire: for the lonely returner, an immovable huddle can signal "we
are a connected group, and you are not part of it."
How the Author Uses It: Explains why teammates stopped running to the penalty area (no rule changed — the
behaviour changed with the formation); indicts England 2020's static reaction.
Related Concepts: The longest walk, belonging, centre-circle position.
```

```
Concept Name: Protecting the Penalty Spot (and de-escalation)
Definition: Teammates guarding the untouched spot so the taker can gather themselves away from the chaos —
best done with de-escalating finesse, not dominance.
Why It Matters: A functional + psychological defence against spot-scuffing (Ch.3), but it backfires when
crude (it invites or manufactures confrontation).
How the Author Uses It: Filipe Luís's elegant one-man guard; the 2022 World Cup swarm fiascos (Argentina/
Saudi, Portugal/Uruguay, Ghana/Uruguay); Kieffer Moore's friendly de-escalation; the good-bouncer research.
Related Concepts: Protecting the taker, gardening the spot (Ch.3), the decoy.
```

```
Concept Name: The Decoy Penalty Taker
Definition: A non-taker holds the ball and behaves like the kicker, absorbing all the opponents' mind games,
then hands off to the real (unbothered) taker once the referee has cleared the area.
Why It Matters: A genuine 2022 innovation — "fight distraction with distraction," protect by misdirection,
and stay unpredictable for the scouting goalkeeper.
How the Author Uses It: Azpilicueta → Havertz (2022 Club World Cup final); copied worldwide; Arsenal's
five-takers-in-seven-penalties variation, which Ødegaard says emerged organically and needs-based ("we
haven't spoken about this… it just happened automatically… it's up to him what he needs").
Related Concepts: Protecting the taker, opponent analysis (Ch.3), variation.
```

```
Concept Name: Contagious Celebration (pride as a weapon)
Definition: An elaborate post-goal pride display (arms wide/up, chest out, chin up) that spreads emotion —
lifting teammates and deflating opponents.
Why It Matters: It is not mere expression: celebration is statistically linked to winning the shootout and to
the *next opponent missing*; observing pride changes others' anticipated emotions and expectations.
How the Author Uses It: Maradona 1990; Rapinoe's iconic 2019 pose; his own celebration study + the four-
experiment pride-contagion replication.
Related Concepts: Emotional contagion, reputation (Ch.3), centre-circle celebration.
```

```
Concept Name: The Goalkeeper Handoff
Definition: The team's goalkeeper takes charge of the ball and personally hands it to the next kicker,
standing between the taker and the goal so the taker sees a teammate, not the opposing keeper.
Why It Matters: Creates a felt two-against-one at the start of the duel and a measure of control — but there
are no guarantees (worked for England 2018, failed 2020) and it can be over-done.
How the Author Uses It: England 2018/2020; Liverpool's Alisson (2022 FA Cup, direct hand + gloved pat);
Naeher (rapid) vs. Mušović (chatty) — the need to tailor.
Related Concepts: Protecting the taker, collective penalty, tailoring support.
```

```
Concept Name: Progressive Centre-Circle Position (territorial dominance)
Definition: Standing at the *front edge* of the centre circle (8–9 m closer to goal) rather than the halfway
line — a shorter walk, closer support, and a first-mover territorial claim that forces opponents to react.
Why It Matters: "You're bossing the pitch. You're in charge" — a low-cost initiative move; also, staying
still and frozen makes no physiological or psychological sense after 120 minutes.
How the Author Uses It: Netherlands U21 2007, Zulte Waregem 2017, NY City FC 2021, Arsenal 2023-24; plus the
case for *movement* (dynamic formation, breaking free to celebrate).
Related Concepts: Marking territory (Ch.3), collective penalty, celebration.
```

```
Concept Name: The Give-and-Take of Penalties (the right kind of giver)
Definition: A penalty is a valuable, tradable "commodity"; gifting it to a teammate can be strategically
optimal — if you are a *strategic* giver, not an indiscriminate one.
Why It Matters: Applies Adam Grant's giver/taker/matcher model — givers are both the top *and* bottom
performers; the difference is strategic, self-protective giving.
How the Author Uses It: Haaland gifting penalties (Szoboszlai, Hwang, Sancho, Gündoğan) — celebrated by some,
condemned by Guardiola/Nicol ("the best taker must take it").
Related Concepts: Belonging, reciprocity, short-term vs. long-term optimisation.
```

## 3. Key Claims

```
Claim: More than half of shootout-winning teams missed at least one penalty.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: 49 of 93 shootouts (52.7%) in the men's WC + Euros + European Cup/CL to 2022 were won by
a team that missed one or more.
Strength of Support: Strong (full-population count for those competitions).
```

```
Claim: Collective support at the moment of a miss reduces pressure and improves later performance.
Type: Empirical / Interpretive
Evidence Provided: Social-support studies (availability alone lowers physiological stress; received support
lowers cortisol; secure relationships lower anxiety most); the Dutch U21s rating the pickup plan their most
valuable prep; Italy's Chiellini/Florenzi vs. England's static reaction.
Strength of Support: Moderate. The mechanism studies are solid; the penalty-specific link is
case-based/inferred, not a controlled test.
```

```
Claim: Elite performers under pressure respond to errors rather than merely avoiding them (HRO model).
Type: Interpretive (transferred evidence)
Evidence Provided: HRO research — ER/aviation/firefighting workers make as many mistakes but don't let them
incapacitate them; 70% of aircraft accidents stem from human error.
Strength of Support: Moderate. Well-established in HRO literature; the transfer to penalties is argued, not tested.
```

```
Claim: Positive thinking is the wrong aim for pressure performance.
Type: Prescriptive / Interpretive
Evidence Provided: Jordet's own failed 2005 "think positive" approach vs. successful 2007 "missing is
natural" approach (with the Dutch winning a 32-kick shootout).
Strength of Support: Moderate. Persuasive practitioner narrative; confounded (many variables changed; n of shootouts tiny).
```

```
Claim: Elaborate post-goal celebration is linked to winning the shootout and to the next opponent missing.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Jordet's study (WC + Euros shootouts pre-2008, tied-at-kick only): significant link;
82% of both-hands-up celebrators were on the winning team; celebration significantly raised the next
opponent's miss chance; slight indication the next teammate scored more.
Strength of Support: Moderate-to-Strong (significant), but the sample is "necessarily quite small" (tied-kick restriction).
```

```
Claim: Observing an opponent's pride display worsens goalkeepers' expected performance (and a teammate's lifts it).
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Four experiments (German + Dutch researchers): experienced keepers observing opponent
pride expected to feel worse/more stressed/less confident; outfielders observing teammate pride expected the opposite.
Strength of Support: Moderate-to-Strong (controlled, four experiments, but anticipated — not actual — performance).
```

```
Claim: The strongest predictor of bar aggression is the ABSENCE of doormen — but aggressive bouncers increase trouble.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Bouncer research — absence of security is the strongest aggression predictor; aggressive/
unreasonable bouncers increase incidents; effective ones are firm-but-friendly, verbally skilled, early-
intervening, team-based, force-as-last-resort.
Strength of Support: Moderate (cited multi-study body), transferred to spot-protection by analogy.
```

```
Claim: The collective strategies work but carry no guarantees and backfire when crude.
Type: Interpretive
Evidence Provided: Keeper handoff (England 2018 won, 2020 lost); spot-protection swarms (Ghana lost, both
went home); Netherlands 2022 did all the celebration/movement and still lost to Argentina.
Strength of Support: Strong as a hedge (multiple counter-examples supplied by the author himself).
```

## 4. Frameworks, Models, and Mental Models

```
Name: The Collective Penalty Toolkit (six sub-strategies)
Description: The modern team-based approach to a penalty.
Components: (1) Protect the spot; (2) protect the taker; (3) the decoy taker; (4) contagious celebration;
(5) the goalkeeper handoff; (6) the centre-circle position (and movement).
How It Works: Each assigns teammates functional/emotional roles to raise the taker's readiness and manage
opponents, referee and tempo.
When It Is Useful: Any in-game penalty or shootout at a level where opponents actively disrupt.
Limitations: No guarantees; each backfires if crude (swarms invite confrontation; over-chatty handoff can
disrupt; celebration can be matched by the opponent).
```

```
Name: High-Reliability-Organisation Error Management
Description: A culture that prioritises the *response* to errors over their avoidance.
Components: Expect failure; respond mindfully/flexibly/dynamically (not automatically/rigidly); non-punitive;
directed at system/culture/communication, not blame.
How It Works: Errors are inevitable, so resilience comes from fast, careful recovery and learning, not from
zero-defect fantasies.
When It Is Useful: Any high-stakes domain with irreducible error (medicine, aviation — and penalties).
Limitations: A transferred model; penalty-specific validation is by practitioner outcome, not experiment.
```

```
Name: Grant's Giver / Taker / Matcher (+ the strategic giver)
Description: A reciprocity-style taxonomy.
Components: Takers (extract), matchers (reciprocate), givers (give more than they get). Givers are the top
performers — and the bottom (indiscriminate givers get exploited).
How It Works: Strategic, self-protective giving builds long-term reciprocity (Haaland gives to get passes
back, but "fights for it" when the team needs him to take).
When It Is Useful: Distributing a scarce, valuable opportunity (a penalty) within a team.
Limitations: Contested case-by-case (Guardiola/Nicol: "the best taker must take it"); short- vs. long-term tension.
```

```
Name: The Good-Bouncer De-escalation Model (for spot protection)
Description: How to hold contested territory without escalating conflict.
Components: Firm-but-friendly; good verbal skills; early intervention; teamwork; physical force as last resort.
How It Works: Presence deters (absence of security is the top aggression predictor) but aggression escalates;
de-escalation (Kieffer Moore's hugs-and-smiles) holds the line without a fight.
When It Is Useful: Guarding the penalty spot amid protests/chaos.
Limitations: Analogy from bar research; height/authority (Moore's 1.96 m) may confound.
```

```
Name: Pride-Expression Emotional Contagion
Description: A prototypical pride display (expanded posture, head back, arms raised, small smile) transmits
emotion to observers.
Components: Expresser's display → observers' anticipated emotions/confidence/expectations shift.
How It Works: Teammates catch confidence; opponents catch its inverse (more stress, lower expectations).
When It Is Useful: Post-goal in a shootout — celebrate to influence the next kicker on both sides.
Limitations: Lab measures *anticipated* not actual performance; opponents can celebrate back (Argentina 2022).
```

## 6. Experiments

```
Experiment Name: Pride-expression contagion (four experiments)
Setup: Experienced footballers observe penalty takers with pride vs. neutral expressions.
Participants: Experienced goalkeepers and outfield players.
Procedure: Show opponent/teammate pride displays; measure anticipated emotions, confidence, performance expectations.
Result: Keepers seeing opponent pride expected to feel worse/more stressed/less confident; outfielders seeing
teammate pride expected to feel better/more confident.
Interpretation: Pride displays transmit emotion — deflating opponents, lifting teammates.
What It Demonstrates: Celebration is a causal social signal, not just personal release.
Potential Alternative Explanation: Anticipated ≠ actual performance; demand characteristics of watching staged displays.
```

```
Experiment Name: Jordet's naive-2005 vs. informed-2007 field "experiment" (natural, uncontrolled)
Setup: Two Dutch-team preparation regimes with different framings of failure.
Participants: Netherlands U20 (2005) and U21 (2007).
Procedure: 2005 — "think positive," only scoring videos; 2007 — "missing is natural," HRO error-response,
the "we ALL walk over" pickup plan.
Result: 2005 lost a 24-kick shootout to Nigeria; 2007 won a record 32-kick shootout vs. England; players
rated the pickup plan the most valuable element.
Interpretation: Normalising the miss + collective pickup beats positive-thinking framing.
What It Demonstrates: A practitioner case for error-response over error-avoidance framing.
Potential Alternative Explanation: n = 2 shootouts; countless uncontrolled differences (players, opponents, luck).
```

## 7. Cases and Stories

```
Case Title: Rashford's longest walk vs. Italy's pickup (Euro 2020 final)
People / Organization: Marcus Rashford, Harry Kane, Kalvin Phillips (England) vs. Chiellini & Florenzi (Italy)
Context: The 2020 Euro final shootout (328m TV audience); the rule keeps non-takers in the centre circle.
What Happened: When Rashford (and earlier Sancho/Saka) missed, England's players stayed interlocked and
immovable — Phillips started toward Rashford twice but stopped when no one followed; Kane (captain, already
scored) stood hidden. When Italy's Belotti missed, Chiellini and Florenzi broke free to walk him back in.
Outcome: England lost; the static reaction "showed" Sancho and Saka their teammates wouldn't be there — both missed.
Concept Illustrated: The longest walk; the shoulder-to-shoulder trap; belonging vs. its absence.
Why This Case Is Useful: The chapter's emotional anchor; a side-by-side natural contrast.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Jordet's Dutch consultancy — naive 2005 to winning 2007
People / Organization: Geir Jordet; Foppe de Haan; the Netherlands U20/U21; Marco van Basten's senior squad
Context: Jordet's first real-world practitioner test (2005–2008), starting with a humiliating loss.
What Happened: A polished 2005 "think positive" talk preceded a 24-kick shootout loss to Nigeria; Jordet
re-learned by interviewing every player, imported the HRO/"missing is natural" model, and in 2007 added the
"we ALL walk over" pickup plan. Van Nistelrooy and van der Sar (the most proficient) sought him out for more.
Outcome: A record 32-kick shootout win over England (2007 U21 Euros); players rated the pickup plan most valuable.
Concept Illustrated: HRO error-response; normalising the miss; the high-achiever's "want to be better" mindset.
Why This Case Is Useful: The origin of the chapter's central lesson; humanising and instructive.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Alan Ball picks up Joe Royle (1970 — the first major shootout)
People / Organization: Everton vs. Borussia Mönchengladbach, European Cup, November 1970
Context: The first shootout in a major international tournament; Netzer reportedly refused to take one.
What Happened: After Royle's saved first kick left him sunk to the ground, Alan Ball (1966 world champion,
next to shoot) ran into the penalty area, embraced him, "hold it up," and escorted him back — as did
Mönchengladbach players for Laumen.
Outcome: A natural era of teammate support (also Littbarski/Stielike 1982; Roberts/Archibald 1984) that has
since faded with the interlocked formation.
Concept Illustrated: Belonging; "it's about being there" (Royle: "the words don't matter much").
Why This Case Is Useful: Historical proof the supportive instinct is native and was later suppressed.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: Filipe Luís guards the spot (Flamengo)
People / Organization: Filipe Luís & Gabi Barbosa, Flamengo (from July 2019)
Context: Brazilian penalties bring 4–5 minutes of chaos and spot-scuffing.
What Happened: Luís — who never took a penalty in his career — stood astride the spot guarding it, gently
pushing opponents back, while Barbosa waited outside; once the referee cleared the area, Luís left and
Barbosa placed the ball on an untouched spot.
Outcome: An elegant one-man protection that keeps the taker out of the chaos.
Concept Illustrated: Protecting the spot; a functional role for a non-taker.
Why This Case Is Useful: The clean template before the crude swarm versions.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: Spot-protection swarms backfire (2022 World Cup)
People / Organization: Argentina/Saudi Arabia; Portugal/Uruguay; Ghana/Uruguay
Context: A 2022 outbreak of spot destruction led teams to cordon the spot — often clumsily.
What Happened: Cordons invited or manufactured confrontations — push-and-shove around Messi (still scored);
Gonçalo Ramos's "pile of gold" guard (Bruno Fernandes scored); Ghana's six-man "Roman shield wall" that
provoked Uruguay, drew Núñez a yellow, and preceded Ayew's saved kick.
Outcome: Ghana lost and both teams went home; Argentina later adapted to stay back and intervene only when needed.
Concept Illustrated: Protecting the spot backfiring when crude; the need for finesse.
Why This Case Is Useful: Vivid counter-examples; the "no guarantees" hedge in action.
Potential for Reuse: Medium
```

```
Case Title: Kieffer Moore de-escalates (Wales vs. USA, 2022)
People / Organization: Kieffer Moore (Wales), protecting Gareth Bale's penalty
Context: Six US players swarmed the referee/spot in protest.
What Happened: Moore (1.96 m) congratulated Bale, then held the spot with flexibility and friendliness —
gently pushing opponents away "always with a comment and a smile," even handing out hugs.
Outcome: Classic de-escalation; Bale calmly equalised.
Concept Illustrated: The good-bouncer de-escalation model applied to spot protection.
Why This Case Is Useful: A concrete, imitable style; bridges to the bouncer research.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Henderson protects Salah (and Kane) — the taker as a team event
People / Organization: Jordan Henderson (Liverpool/England), Mo Salah, Harry Kane; vs. Emi Martínez / France
Context: Liverpool learned from Man Utd's sluggish failure to protect Bruno Fernandes vs. Martínez.
What Happened: Henderson escorted Salah out of Martínez's reach, screened him with teammates, lingered
"fixing his socks" to hold the screen, and stayed with him — five Liverpool players had functional roles;
Salah scored. Henderson also grabbed the ball and shielded Kane before England's first France penalty (Kane
scored); when Henderson was subbed off before the second, Kane was left alone at the spot for 30s and missed.
Outcome: Protection works when proactive/concerted (Henderson on) and falters when reactive (Henderson off).
Concept Illustrated: Protecting the taker; the penalty as a collective event; the non-taker's leadership role.
Why This Case Is Useful: The clearest demonstration that support is functional, not just sentimental.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: The decoy penalty taker is born (Azpilicueta → Havertz, 2022)
People / Organization: César Azpilicueta & Kai Havertz (Chelsea) vs. Palmeiras, Club World Cup final
Context: Chelsea knew Palmeiras crowded the taker; a game-deciding penalty with ~7 minutes left.

## 11. Tensions, Contradictions, and Open Questions

```
Issue: Stoic process-orientation (Team GB, Ch.2) vs. the belonging/pickup imperative (Ch.4).
Author's Position: England 2020's static reaction may have been a deliberate "don't react to outcomes" plan —
but Jordet judges it a lost chance for belonging, contradicted by the same players celebrating goals/opponent misses.
Possible Counterargument: A consistent no-reaction policy (to success and failure alike) is defensible and processual.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Whether teams with active miss-pickup routines outperform stoic ones, controlled for quality.

Author's Position: Reports a significant celebration→winning link.
Possible Counterargument: The tied-at-kick restriction makes the sample "necessarily quite small"; correlational.

## 13. Psychology Connections

  belonging threat — the chapter's psychological foundation.

- **Emotional contagion (pride)**: celebration transmits confidence to teammates and its inverse to opponents.
- **Error management / high-reliability psychology**: respond mindfully vs. rigidly; non-punitive error culture.
- **Reappraisal / acceptance (Ch.1–2 callback)**: "missing is natural" as reframing threat as normal.
- **Status & leadership**: high-status members' support carries more weight (Mbappé, Messi, Renard); "leadership in actions."
- **Reciprocity styles (Grant)**: givers/takers/matchers; the exploited "doormat" giver.
- **De-escalation psychology**: firm-but-friendly beats dominance; personal-space and hostility as escalators.

## 14. Mathematics and Decision Science Connections

- **Base rates**: 52.7% of shootouts won despite a miss — reframes the decision value of a single miss.
- **Expected value of gifting a penalty**: Haaland's give-and-take is a team-EV vs. individual-EV trade
  (Guardiola's "best taker must take it" is a pure per-kick EV argument; Grant's case is repeated-game EV).

- **Hidden information / misdirection (game theory)**: the decoy taker manipulates the goalkeeper's opponent
  model; Arsenal's five-takers variation raises the keeper's prediction entropy.

  "no guarantees" framing.
- **First-mover advantage**: the progressive centre-circle position as a low-cost initiative claim.

## 16. AI and Machine Learning Connections

*(All inferred unless noted.)*
- **Graceful failure & incident response (strong bridge)**: the HRO error-response culture is exactly the
  ethos of resilient systems — assume failure, respond fast, run blameless post-mortems, fix the
  system/process not the individual. "Respond, don't just avoid" is a systems-reliability principle.
- **Multi-agent cooperation & reward sharing**: Haaland's strategic giving is a reward-allocation problem in
  a cooperative multi-agent team — local EV loss for global/long-run gain (credit assignment, shaping).

  propagation and signalling in multi-agent systems.
- **Ensemble robustness / social support**: distributing functional roles (protectors, handoff, decoy)

- **Closed-loop control / BCI (Neuro 11)**: brainwave-feedback training toward an "optimal mental state" is
