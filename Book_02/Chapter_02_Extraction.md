## 1. Chapter Thesis

Where Chapter 1 mapped how pressure is *felt* and makes players choke, Chapter 2 is the counter-move: how
players regain a **sense of control** over the penalty situation and themselves. The chapter first
dismantles the coaches' favourite excuse — the penalty shootout as a "lottery" or "Russian roulette" —
by showing that overall footballing skill is a surprisingly *small* factor (the best players in the world,
Messi included, are average-to-below-average penalty takers), which means the shootout is a leveller, not
a talent test, and control has to be found elsewhere. It then catalogues the concrete levers of control:
choosing a penalty *technique* (goalkeeper-independent vs. goalkeeper-dependent, a trade-off of short-term
comfort for long-term control), building a stable *pre-shot routine*, using deliberate *ball placement*
and a *post-whistle pause* to seize the tempo from referee and goalkeeper, *breathing* to down-regulate the
nervous system, and adopting a *mindset of acceptance* — performing not by being fearless but by acting
despite fear. The chapter closes with the England 2018 case, a systematic institutional effort to convert
a decades-long "lottery" belief into collective *perceived control*, and thereby break a 22-year shootout
curse. The throughline: control in a penalty is mostly *perceived* control, and perceived control can be
engineered.

## 2. Key Concepts

```
Concept Name: Perceived Control (sense of control)
Definition: The belief that an outcome can be influenced by your own effort — i.e., that a shootout is not
(entirely) a lottery. It is distinct from actual control over the result.
Why It Matters: It is the chapter's central variable and the antidote to choking. In Jordet's examinations
of elite takers, players who believed a shootout was all about luck were more likely to experience harmful
anxiety than those who said it was all about skill; those who did not rate their own penalty skill also
felt more anxiety.
How the Author Uses It: The organising thread — every technique in the chapter (routine, ball placement,
pause, breath, mindset) is reframed as a way to manufacture perceived control, culminating in England's
explicit programme to raise "the level of perceived control for the players and the staff."
Related Concepts: The lottery metaphor, learned helplessness, process over outcome, self-efficacy.
```

```
Concept Name: The Lottery / Russian-Roulette Metaphor (and its harm)
Definition: The widespread framing of the shootout as pure luck — "penalties are a lottery" (Capello, van
Gaal, Hiddink, Murphy); Karembeu's darker "loading a bullet into the chamber… someone will get the bullet."
Why It Matters: The metaphor is not neutral. It is "the resignation of control," and believing it is
statistically associated with more harmful anxiety. It also, at institutional scale, "takes ownership away
from the players" and breeds helplessness.
How the Author Uses It: As the belief to be dismantled — first empirically (skill matters little, but
control levers exist), then institutionally (England had to actively kill the lottery narrative its last
five managers had voiced).
Related Concepts: Perceived control, action bias, the one place Jordet *concedes* luck (see §9).
```

```
Concept Name: Skill as a Leveller ("The Messi problem")
Definition: Overall footballing skill plays a real but relatively small role in penalty conversion, so
elite outfield talent does not translate into elite penalty records — everyone starts a shootout with
roughly the same chance.
Why It Matters: It rebuts the lottery-metaphor's implicit premise (that only luck separates takers) *and*
the meritocratic premise (that the best player will score). If skill barely moves the needle, then the
controllable, trainable psychological/technical levers matter proportionally more.
How the Author Uses It: A benchmarking tour — Messi (77.9%) and a long roster of star scorers cluster near
or below the ~78-82% league average; multiple datasets show position, market value and skill proxies
barely predict outcome; team strength shows a small effect that may be pressure-handling, not skill.
Related Concepts: Base rate, the specialists (the exceptions), perceived control.
```

```
Concept Name: Goalkeeper-Independent Technique
Definition: The taker decides in advance where and how to shoot, then executes regardless of the keeper.
Why It Matters: The default method for most players. It simplifies the task and is easy to execute
*adequately* but hard to execute *exceptionally*; its Achilles heel is that the one thing the taker cannot
control — which way the keeper dives — nearly halves the odds when the keeper guesses right.
How the Author Uses It: Exemplified by Harry Kane and Dominik Szoboszlai, whose sheer striking quality lets
them beat the odds even when the keeper goes the right way (Kane still scores 81% then, vs. most players' 54%).
Related Concepts: Goalkeeper-dependent technique, aim-high heuristic, action bias, perceived control.
```

```
Concept Name: Goalkeeper-Dependent Technique
Definition: The taker watches the keeper's movement immediately before and during the kick and sends the
ball wherever the keeper isn't — letting the keeper effectively "decide" the shot.
Why It Matters: It trades away *initial* control (you don't pre-commit; you may have only tenths of a
second to read, decide and strike, often without looking at the ball) for far more *ultimate* control. In
the chapter's meta-lesson, accepting short-term vulnerability can buy better outcomes — but only if paired
with elite skill and a productive mental state.
How the Author Uses It: Lewandowski is the archetype (94% on 62 such kicks; only average when he
pre-commits). Maradona described it plainly ("I stand on my leg and I watch the keeper").
Related Concepts: Goalkeeper-independent technique, the initial-vs-ultimate-control trade-off, deception
(the keeper's counter-feint).
```

```
Concept Name: Pre-Shot / Pre-Performance Routine
Definition: A stable, rehearsed sequence of behaviours before the kick (walk-back, stance, breaths,
gaze, run-up) that the player repeats identically under any pressure.
Why It Matters: Routines channel attention, minimise distraction, increase the feeling of control and
reduce anxiety; a meta-analysis of 61 studies found a significant, robust effect on performance. There is
no single correct routine — what matters is comfort and rehearsal.
How the Author Uses It: Kane as the "shining role model" (near-identical routine across a decade and two
leagues); Ødegaard's deliberate, trained version ("I command the situation").
Related Concepts: Ball placement, post-whistle pause, automaticity (Ch.1), control.
```

```
Concept Name: Post-Whistle Pause
Definition: A deliberate, self-paced delay between the referee's whistle and the start of the run-up.
Why It Matters: Historically takers instinctively move in under a second (treating the whistle as a
starting gun); a deliberate 3–5+ second pause grounds the taker, hands them tempo control over keeper and
referee, and lets them line the shot up. It has a *Goldilocks shape*, not a "longer is better" one: those
who rush miss more, **and** those "caught overthinking" who wait too long also suffer — the pause is an
*owned optimum*. It must also be genuinely chosen — forcing a pause a player doesn't want can *add* stress
(elite archers made to wait 5 extra seconds performed drastically worse).
How the Author Uses It: Endō's historic 6.4-second stillness at the 2010 World Cup; Borges's father's
maxim ("the whistle is not a signal to run, it's a signal to start your ritual"); England telling players
"the whistle is the referee's cue, not yours."
Related Concepts: Reaction time / speeding up (Ch.1), perceived control, the two ways to choke.
```

```
Concept Name: Diaphragmatic Breathing / the Physiological Sigh
Definition: Slow, belly-led breaths (stomach rather than chest moving) that activate the parasympathetic
nervous system and induce relaxation; the "physiological sigh" is breathing with the exhale roughly twice
as long as the inhale.
Why It Matters: A portable down-regulation tool. Shallow upper-chest breathing does the opposite —
activating the sympathetic "fight or flight" system. Best rehearsed away from performance so it becomes
the default fallback.
How the Author Uses It: Ronaldo's three visible deep breaths (2006), Lewandowski's and Borges's routines,
plus Djokovic and Huberman as cross-domain support.
Related Concepts: Post-whistle pause, mindset/acceptance, acute-stress cascade (Ch.1).
```

```
Concept Name: Acceptance Mindset (act despite fear; attend, don't feel)
Definition: Performing under pressure is not being fearless or comfortable — it is acting directly in the
face of fear and discomfort, treating anxiety as normal/natural/sometimes useful, and shifting focus from
how you feel to what you attend to.
Why It Matters: It reframes nerves from a problem to be eliminated into a companion to be accepted, freeing
attention for the task. Haberl: "I don't care how you feel; I care about what you attend to… the function
of the feeling, not the form."
How the Author Uses It: Haaland's candid "I was shit nervous"; Zidane vomiting twice seconds before
scoring (2004); Djokovic's "storm inside"; Rotella and Phil Jackson ("being aware is more important than
being smart").
Related Concepts: Threat vs. challenge appraisal (Ch.1), mindfulness, perceived control, present-focus.
```

```
Concept Name: Process Over Outcome / Ownership
Definition: Directing attention and effort to a controllable process (the routine, the phases, the roles)
rather than the uncontrollable result, and giving players ownership of that process.
Why It Matters: It is the psychological mechanism by which "perceived control" is actually installed — if
the shootout is a skill with an improvable process, it stops being a lottery.
How the Author Uses It: Southgate's signature "focus on the process, not the outcome," operationalised by
Markham's England programme (breaking the shootout into trainable phases the players own).
Related Concepts: Perceived control, the four shootout phases, dosage / paralysis by analysis.
```

## 3. Key Claims

```
Claim: Believing a shootout is luck (vs. skill) is associated with more harmful anxiety.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Jordet's psychological examinations of elite shootout takers; those who believed it was
all luck, and those who didn't rate their own penalty skill, experienced more anxiety.
Strength of Support: Moderate (correlational, own research, sample/method not detailed in-chapter).
```

```
Claim: Overall footballing skill plays only a relatively small role in penalty conversion.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Messi 77.9% (below the ~78-82% averages); star scorers cluster near/below average;
position differences shrink from shootout data (forwards 80% vs. defenders 67.7%) to near-nil in large
in-game datasets (Primeira Liga 833 kicks: 79.6/78.7/75.4%; InStat 100,000+: 76/75-76/73-74/72%); a study
of 1711 kicks found market value non-significant (74.9/74.8/73.2%).
Strength of Support: Strong (convergent across multiple independent datasets).
```

```
Claim: Players on stronger teams convert somewhat more penalties, but this may not be pure skill.
Type: Interpretive
Evidence Provided: 1711-kick study: stronger teams 76.4% vs. weaker 71.2%; 1067-shootout cup study
(2004-05 to 2017-18, 14 competitions): favourites won 52.5%, outsiders 47.5%.
Strength of Support: Moderate. Jordet explicitly flags that better-team players "may also be better under
pressure," so the effect is not automatically attributable to skill.
```

```
Claim: The highest-scoring shot direction is straight down the middle.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Jordet's major-shootout data: left 70.7%, right 71.2%, middle 77.9% — because keepers
have an action bias to dive to a side.
Strength of Support: Moderate-to-Strong (own dataset; consistent with the action-bias literature).
```

```
Claim: When the keeper guesses the correct side, a goalkeeper-independent taker's odds nearly halve.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Major-shootout data: keeper wrong side → 91% scored; keeper correct side → 54% scored.
This is the one place Jordet says the "lottery" proponents have a point.
Strength of Support: Strong (large effect in own data); note the concession that some genuine luck exists.
```

```
Claim: Goalkeeper-dependent technique can yield elite conversion — for those with the requisite skill.
Type: Empirical / Prescriptive
Evidence Provided: Lewandowski 94% on 62 GK-dependent kicks (ball to the keeper's dived side only 16% of
the time) vs. only 81% (average) on 21 pre-committed kicks; Maradona 93.1% at Napoli.
Strength of Support: Moderate (compelling case evidence; explicitly contingent on "highly specialised skill").
```

```
Claim: A distinct pre-performance routine improves performance under pressure.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Studies showing routines minimise distraction, raise felt control and reduce anxiety; a
meta-analysis of 61 studies across sports found a significant and robust effect.
Strength of Support: Strong (meta-analytic), though "robust effect on performance" is stated without effect size.
```

```
Claim: Taking more time to physically place the ball is associated with scoring more.
Type: Empirical
Evidence Provided: Jordet's study on preparation times: players who took longer placing the ball scored more.
Strength of Support: Moderate (correlational; "could indicate" — hedged by the author).
```

```
Claim: A deliberate pause helps, but only if genuinely chosen and within an owned optimum; both rushing and
over-waiting hurt.
Type: Empirical / Prescriptive
Evidence Provided: Leading takers now pause 3–5s; historically takers rush (<1s) and miss more; those who
overthink and wait too long also suffer; a study of elite archers forced to wait 5 seconds beyond their
usual time showed drastic negative performance effects.
Strength of Support: Moderate (the archery study is the load-bearing evidence for the forced-pause downside).
```

```
Claim: Diaphragmatic breathing down-regulates arousal (parasympathetic); shallow chest breathing up-regulates it.
Type: Empirical / Theoretical
Evidence Provided: Physiological account (parasympathetic vs. sympathetic activation); Huberman's
"physiological sigh" (5 min, exhale twice the inhale) ameliorating anxiety.
Strength of Support: Moderate (established physiology invoked; penalty-specific effect is inferential).
```

```
Claim: Performing under pressure is acting despite fear, not the absence of fear.
Type: Interpretive / Prescriptive
Evidence Provided: Haaland's candor; Zidane vomiting twice then scoring; Rotella, Haberl, Djokovic, Jackson.
Strength of Support: Moderate (persuasive cases + expert testimony; not a controlled test).
```

```
Claim: England's 2018 shootout win was produced by a systematic effort to raise perceived control.
Type: Interpretive (case-based)
Evidence Provided: The Markham programme (18 months, ~100 papers, phase model, player/referee analysis,
dosage, routines, roles); prior record of five straight shootout losses since 1996 and England's
league-leading avoidance coping.
Strength of Support: Moderate. A single successful case; Jordet presents it as exemplary rather than as
proof, and Markham himself notes timing/being "ahead of the curve" as part of it.
```

## 4. Frameworks, Models, and Mental Models

```
Name: The Two Penalty Techniques (Independent vs. Dependent)
Description: A taxonomy of how a taker relates to the keeper's movement.
Components: (1) Goalkeeper-independent — pre-decide and execute; (2) Goalkeeper-dependent — read the keeper
and go the other way.
How It Works: Independent trades away the ability to counter a correct guess for simplicity and early
commitment; dependent sacrifices early comfort/control to exploit the keeper's late commitment.
When It Is Useful: Independent for takers with exceptional striking power/accuracy (Kane, Szoboszlai);
dependent for those with the perception, nerve and skill to read and adapt in tenths of a second (Lewandowski).
Technique is a *toggle, not a fixed identity*: "variation helps penalty takers keep ahead of the
opposition" — Kane switched to a goalkeeper-dependent kick vs. Arsenal (2023-24 CL QF) after ~80
goalkeeper-independent penalties, and Lewandowski still takes ~21 pre-committed kicks (81%).
Limitations: Independent is exposed when the keeper guesses right (odds ~halve); dependent is beatable by a
well-timed keeper feint (Bono's left-right-left on Haaland) and demands elite skill.
```

```
Name: The Initial-vs-Ultimate-Control Trade-off (the chapter's meta-lesson)
Description: Being prepared to accept some initial vulnerability and sacrifice short-term control and
comfort can be an excellent way to gain more control and better outcomes ultimately.
Components: Short-term comfort/control (given up) → contingent skill + productive mental state → greater
ultimate control.
How It Works: Generalised from the goalkeeper-dependent technique; letting the keeper move first feels
worse but wins more.
When It Is Useful: Any pressured decision where premature commitment forecloses better information.
Limitations: Author-stated: "contingent on having a highly specialised skill, and being able to place
yourself in a productive mental state at the moment of the performance."
```

```
Name: Pre-Performance Routine (components)
Description: A repeatable behavioural sequence before execution.
Components: Ball placement; walk-back; stance/set-up; breathing; gaze (ball vs. keeper); post-whistle
pause; run-up (steps, angle, pace).
How It Works: Standardising these channels attention onto controllables and buffers against distraction.
When It Is Useful: Any self-paced, high-pressure execution task.
Limitations: No single correct configuration; a routine adopted only because a coach mandated it can add
stress; must be rehearsed and personally owned.
```

```
Name: The Four Phases of the Penalty Shootout (Jordet's model, used by England)
Description: The shootout decomposed into functional units so each can be trained.
Components: Phase 1 — the break after extra time; Phase 2 — the centre circle; Phase 3 — the walk; Phase 4
— at the penalty spot.
How It Works: Turns an overwhelming event into a sequence of ownable, improvable sub-tasks — "there is a
process that we can actually make that will take the pressure away from the players."
When It Is Useful: Designing training and role/communication structures around a high-stakes team event.
Limitations: Cited from Jordet's own paper; presented as influential rather than empirically decisive here.
```

```
Name: England's "Perception of Control" Programme (Markham, 2017–2018)
Description: A multi-strand institutional intervention to convert a "lottery" belief into trained control.
Components: Southgate presentation & prioritisation; dosage (avoid "paralysis by analysis"); phase model;
process/ownership; own-player analysis with a pressure rating; player workshop & framing; referee meetings;
goalkeeper-bottle intel; whistle re-framing; goalkeeper ball-handoff (counter-distraction); uniform
post-kick support (Team GB hockey inspiration); tailored training & full dress rehearsal; structured roles
and zones in the ET break; huddle-location options.
How It Works: Each strand adds perceived control or removes a source of helplessness/distraction.
When It Is Useful: Institutionalising performance under pressure for a team with a losing psychological history.
Limitations: n = 1 success; timing ("ahead of the curve") credited by Markham; hard to isolate which
strands mattered.
```

## 6. Experiments

```
Experiment Name: The elite-archer forced-delay manipulation
Setup: Elite archers required to delay their shot beyond their habitual timing.
Participants: Elite archers (n not specified).
Procedure: Add a mandatory ~5-second delay beyond each archer's usual pre-shot time.
Result: Drastic negative effect on performance.
Interpretation: Disrupting a self-paced, well-grooved timing harms execution; the *ownership* of timing matters.
What It Demonstrates: A pause helps only when it is the performer's own; imposed pauses can backfire.
Potential Alternative Explanation: The specific 5-second increment may exceed an optimal window rather than
"imposition per se" being the harm; archery timing may not transfer to football.
```

(No controlled experiments on penalty takers are reported in this chapter; the penalty evidence is
observational/archival, consistent with the book's "natural laboratory" stance.)

## 7. Cases and Stories

```
Case Title: The Messi problem — the best player is a below-average penalty taker
People / Organization: Lionel Messi (and a roster of elite scorers)
Context: Benchmarking penalty skill against league averages (~78.6% top-30 leagues; Ligue 1 79.7%).
What Happened: Messi has scored 109 of 140 career penalties (77.9%) — below average. Star scorers cluster
```

## 9. Counterintuitive Insights

```
Insight: The best footballers are mediocre penalty takers.
Common Belief: The most skilful players will convert penalties at elite rates.
Author's Argument: Overall skill barely moves penalty conversion; Messi (77.9%) is below average, and star
scorers cluster near the mean.
Evidence: Career records vs. league averages; position/market-value studies showing negligible differences.
Why It Is Surprising: It divorces "world-class player" from "world-class penalty taker."
```

```
Insight: The best place to shoot is straight down the middle.
Common Belief: Aim for the corners; the middle is where the keeper is.
Author's Argument: Because keepers have an action bias to dive, the middle (where they were standing) scores
most — 77.9% vs. ~71% for either side.
Evidence: Jordet's major-shootout dataset; Panenka's 1976 innovation.
Why It Is Surprising: It recommends shooting where the keeper currently stands.
```

```
Insight: Giving the keeper the first move gives *you* more control.
Common Belief: Commit early and decisively; don't let the opponent dictate.
Author's Argument: The goalkeeper-dependent technique sacrifices initial control to exploit the keeper's
late commitment, yielding higher conversion for skilled takers (Lewandowski 94% vs. 81% when he pre-commits).
Evidence: Lewandowski's and Maradona's records.
Why It Is Surprising: Ceding control is framed as the route to more control.
```

```
Insight: Being predictable can be fine.
Common Belief: You must vary your penalties or the keeper will read you.
Author's Argument: If your execution is good enough (pace + precision), predictability is survivable —
Szoboszlai went left on 14 of 16 kicks and still scored, his one miss beating the keeper.
Evidence: Szoboszlai's record; keeper Nyland's testimony.
Why It Is Surprising: It contradicts the game-theory instinct to randomise.
```

```
Insight: The whistle is not your cue.
Common Belief: The referee's whistle is the signal to take the kick.
Author's Argument: The whistle is the *referee's* cue; the taker can start the run-up whenever they choose —
so the pause is a right, not a delay.
Evidence: Borges's father's maxim; England's explicit instruction to players; Ødegaard, Lewandowski, Endō.
Why It Is Surprising: It reassigns tempo control from the official to the taker.
```

```
Insight: Anxiety is not the enemy — and neither is failure; accept both.
Common Belief: To perform, you must calm down / eliminate nerves / "think positive" — and never plan to fail.
Author's Argument: Accept anxiety as normal and sometimes helpful, and accept that you *will* miss
sometimes (Rapinoe's epigraph: her success comes from "the acceptance that I will miss them"); then attend
to the task, not the feeling ("the function of the feeling, not the form").
Evidence: Rapinoe's epigraph; Haaland's candor; Zidane vomiting then scoring; Djokovic's "storm inside";
Rotella, Haberl, Jackson.
Why It Is Surprising: It rejects the ubiquitous "stay positive / be fearless / never think about missing"
sports cliché — planning to sometimes fail is what frees the performer.
```

## 11. Tensions, Contradictions, and Open Questions

```
Issue: "It's not a lottery" vs. the concession that the keeper's guess nearly halves the odds.
Author's Position: Skill and control matter, but Jordet explicitly concedes that when the keeper guesses
right (an uncontrollable), goalkeeper-independent takers drop from 91% to 54% — "this is where proponents
of the lottery metaphor will get my support."
Possible Counterargument: A large, uncontrollable coin-flip element sits at the core; "control" may be
mostly *perceived*, not real.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Decomposition of variance into taker-skill, keeper-skill, and chance
across many kicks.
```

```
Issue: Aim high vs. shoot down the middle.
Author's Position: He reports both the EPL/Primeira Liga "aim high" advice and his own "middle scores most"
finding without fully reconciling them.
Possible Counterargument: They may not conflict (height vs. horizontal placement), but the chapter doesn't
state a combined rule.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Joint analysis of height × horizontal placement × keeper behaviour.
```

```
Issue: Predictability is survivable (Szoboszlai) vs. secrecy/unpredictability is an edge (Lewandowski).
Author's Position: Both — execution quality can make predictability safe, yet opacity raises scoring odds.
Possible Counterargument: The two claims pull in opposite directions on how much randomisation matters.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Whether predictability costs conversion *controlling for* strike quality.
```

```
Issue: Execution quality vs. un-exploitable randomisation (external: game-theory / minimax).
Author's Position: Jordet leans execution — a predictable taker (Szoboszlai, 14/16 same side) is fine if
the strike is good enough.
Possible Counterargument: The economics-of-penalties / minimax tradition (mixed strategies) argues elite
takers approximate an *un-exploitable randomised* strategy and that predictability is punished — a prime
cross-book collision (feeds book-profile §13/§22).
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Whether exploitable side-bias reduces conversion once strike quality
and keeper scouting are controlled.
```

```
Issue: The England case as evidence.
Author's Position: Presented as the exemplary triumph of engineered perceived control.
Possible Counterargument: n = 1; Markham himself credits timing ("ahead of the curve"); the specific
causal ingredients can't be isolated; subsequent England shootout results are outside this chapter.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Repeated, controlled application across teams/tournaments.
```

```
Issue: Team strength predicts shootout success — skill or pressure-handling?
Author's Position: Reports the effect (76.4% vs. 71.2%; favourites 52.5%) but explicitly refuses to
attribute it to skill alone.
Possible Counterargument: Could be selection (better teams have calmer takers), not a causal team-quality effect.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: Controlling for individual pressure-coping when comparing team tiers.
```

## 13. Psychology Connections

- **Perceived control / self-efficacy & learned helplessness:** the whole chapter is an applied study of
  control beliefs; the "lottery" narrative is essentially induced helplessness, and the interventions
  restore agency.
- **Appraisal theory (threat vs. challenge):** continues Ch.1 — accepting anxiety as normal/helpful is a
  reappraisal move (Rotella's "exciting, beneficial and welcome").
- **Attention & choking:** routines, pauses and mindfulness are attentional-control tools against the
  self-focus/overthinking mechanisms of Ch.1; Haberl's "attend, don't feel."
- **Autonomic regulation:** diaphragmatic breathing / physiological sigh as parasympathetic down-regulation.
- **Action bias (in keepers):** keepers dive rather than stay because standing still risks looking
  foolish/indifferent — an omission/commission asymmetry the taker can exploit.
- **Gambler's fallacy** (named in England's Southgate presentation) as a decision-bias input to shootout strategy.
- **Mindfulness / acceptance (ACT-adjacent):** present-focus, accepting inner "storms," not staying in
  emotions "too long" (Djokovic).

## 14. Mathematics and Decision Science Connections

- **Base rates / benchmarking:** the ~78–82% conversion baseline is the reference class that makes "Messi
  is below average" legible; the whole skill argument is base-rate reasoning.
- **Expected value & risk under keeper behaviour:** middle 77.9% vs. sides ~71%; keeper-correct 54% vs.
  keeper-wrong 91% — an EV surface over shot choice × keeper action.
- **Game theory / mixed strategies:** the predictability (Szoboszlai) vs. unpredictability (Lewandowski's
  secrecy) tension is a mixed-strategy question — when does randomisation pay vs. when does execution
  quality dominate?
- **Information & timing:** goalkeeper-dependent technique = delaying commitment to acquire information
  (the keeper's dive) — value-of-information under a shrinking time budget.
- **Statistical significance & sample size:** market-value differences "not statistically significant";
  Szoboszlai's 10 kicks flagged as "a ridiculously small sample."
- **Signal weighting:** the "pressure rating" is explicit sample-weighting of observations by regime.
- **Near-coin-flip margins:** favourites 52.5% vs. outsiders 47.5% as a small edge over chance.

```
technique, routine, ball placement, the post-whistle pause, breathing, and an acceptance mindset — because
raw skill barely decides the outcome.
```
