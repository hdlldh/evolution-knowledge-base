## Missing Items

1. **The "five common AI debates" list with terse answers** (book p.19): In "The Plan for the Book" section, the authors preview Part Five (Society) with a five-question list: (1) Will there still be jobs? *Yes.* (2) Will this generate more inequality? *Perhaps.* (3) Will a few large companies control everything? *It depends.* (4) Will countries engage in race-to-the-bottom policy-making and forfeit privacy/security for competitive advantage? *Some will.* (5) Will the world end? *You still have plenty of time to derive value from this book.* This is a distinctive, highly quotable structural device — a book-length table of contents compressed into five yes/no-style questions with hedged one-line answers — and was entirely omitted from the extraction.
2. **AI trained on human-generated data learning "treacherous biases and stereotypes"** (book p.19): In the same "Plan for the Book" section, the authors state that "in addition to upside opportunities, AI poses systemic risks that may hit your business unless you take preemptory actions... some prediction machines trained on human-generated data have already 'learned' treacherous biases and stereotypes." This is a specific, consequential claim about AI risk/bias that was omitted from the extraction entirely — notably, it's also the chapter's only direct gesture toward algorithmic bias, a major theme in AI-ethics literature generally, so its omission is a meaningful gap for cross-book comparison purposes.
3. **The claim that judgment's value is the "most significant implication" of prediction machines** (book p.18): "The most significant implication of prediction machines is that they increase the value of judgment." This specific, strong claim — previewing Part Two — was implicitly gestured at in the extraction's discussion of the book's five-part structure but never captured as its own standalone claim.
4. **Kathryn Hume's title/credential** (book p.14): The chapter identifies her as "currently head of digital investments at the Royal Bank of Canada" when introducing the "AI Insight" term. The extraction's Section 7/Section 18 entries cite her by name but drop this credentialing detail, which matters for assessing the authority behind the "AI Insight" term.
5. **The claim that AI tools' downstream impact is often unpredictable in advance** (book p.19): "Sometimes, it's hard to tell in advance when a tool will have such a powerful effect. For example, few people predicted, when they tried it for the first time, that the Google search tool would transform the media industry and become the basis of one of the most valuable companies on earth." This is used as a distinct cautionary example (about the *difficulty of forecasting AI's downstream strategic impact*) separate from the earlier "New Economy" Google search case (which was about search becoming cheap and creating winners/losers) — the extraction's Section 7 New Economy case entry doesn't capture this second, distinct point about unpredictability.

## Overgeneralizations

- The extraction's Claim 4 (Section 3) states the Amazon scenario shows "a sufficiently accurate prediction machine can change... the strategy itself" without reiterating in the claim statement itself that this is explicitly the authors' own hypothetical, not an observed case — the Evidence Provided field does correctly flag this, but a reader skimming only the Claim field could come away thinking this is an established finding rather than a thought experiment.

## Important Nuance Lost

- The book's Key Points summary (book p.19–20) frames prediction machines as something organizations "can exploit... by adopting AI tools to assist with executing their current strategy," with strategy-changing effects as something that "may" motivate change only once tools "become powerful" — a more conditional, staged framing than the extraction's Section 1 thesis statement, which moves more directly from "cheap prediction" to "strategy transformation." The chapter itself is careful to sequence these as two distinct stages (assist → possibly transform), and that sequencing is slightly compressed in the extraction's thesis.
- The chapter explicitly hedges that AI's societal trade-offs "will almost surely be different across different countries and cultures" (book p.20) — this qualification on the Part Five preview wasn't captured, and matters because it signals the authors don't expect universal answers to the "five debates" listed above.

## Potential Disagreements to Track Later

- The offhand claim that "prediction machines trained on human-generated data have already 'learned' treacherous biases and stereotypes" (book p.19) is asserted without a specific citation, study, or example in this chapter. This is worth flagging for later comparison against any book in the knowledge base that treats algorithmic bias as a central topic (with actual case studies, e.g., COMPAS recidivism scoring, facial recognition disparities) — this book's treatment here is a brief aside, not a developed argument, and readers looking for depth on AI bias/fairness should be pointed elsewhere. (The book's own Chapter 20, "Managing AI Risk," may develop this further — worth checking when that chapter is extracted.)
- The book's light-touch, "it depends" / "some will" answers to the five AI-and-society questions (see Additional Cases above) position this book as deliberately non-alarmist and non-utopian relative to both AI-doom and AI-utopia literature — a useful anchor point for cross-book comparison on authorial stance toward AI risk.
