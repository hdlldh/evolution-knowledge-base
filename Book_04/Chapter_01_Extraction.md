## 1. Chapter Thesis

The recent wave of AI advances does not deliver "intelligence" in a general sense — it delivers a large drop in the cost of one specific input: prediction. Because prediction is a well-understood input to decision-making, economics (not computer science) is the right toolkit for understanding what cheaper AI-driven prediction means for business, jobs, and society. The book's approach is to identify trade-offs (e.g., more data for less privacy, more speed for less accuracy, more autonomy for less control) rather than prescribe a single best AI strategy, since the right answer depends on each organization's own weighing of those trade-offs.

## 2. Key Concepts

```
Concept Name: Prediction (as an economic input)
Definition: The process of using existing information (data) to generate new information (about an unknown state, current or future). Framed by the authors as a well-defined economic input distinct from "intelligence" writ large.
Why It Matters: It reframes AI from an amorphous, mysterious technology into a specific, analyzable input whose falling cost can be studied with existing economic tools (the economics of a cost drop).
How the Author Uses It: Used as the organizing lens for the entire book — the authors state their "first key insight" is that new AI is fundamentally a cost drop in prediction, not "intelligence" per se.
Related Concepts: Decision-making, uncertainty, trade-offs
```

```
Concept Name: Trade-offs as the organizing logic of AI strategy
Definition: The idea that AI adoption decisions are not about finding one optimal strategy but about weighing competing costs and benefits — e.g., more data means less privacy, more speed means less accuracy, more autonomy means less control.
Why It Matters: Sets reader expectations for the whole book: it will not be a prescriptive playbook, but a framework for evaluating each organization's specific trade-offs.
How the Author Uses It: Explicitly stated as a Key Point and repeated as the book's stance ("Prediction Machines is not a recipe for success... Instead, we emphasize trade-offs").
Related Concepts: Prediction (as an economic input), decision-making under uncertainty
```

*Additional note (from audit): the chapter also lays out a four-audience value proposition (book p.3) — business leaders get an understanding of AI's impact on management and decisions; students/recent graduates get a framework for thinking about the evolution of jobs and careers; financial analysts/VCs get a structure for investment theses; policymakers get guidelines for how AI may change society and how policy might shape that change. This maps directly onto how different readers should approach the rest of the book.*

## 7. Cases and Stories

```
Case Title: Alexa answering "What's the capital of Delaware?"
People / Organization: Amazon (Alexa)
Context: Opening scenario of the book — a child asks Alexa a question while doing homework, and a parent in another room is caught mid-thought trying to recall the answer.
What Happened: Alexa answers correctly and near-instantly ("The capital of Delaware is Dover") by interpreting natural language and predicting the response the user is seeking, not by "knowing" facts in a human sense.
Outcome: Used to illustrate that Alexa's apparent "intelligence" is actually a prediction process — converting sound to words, then predicting what information those words are seeking.
Concept Illustrated: Prediction as the actual mechanism behind AI that appears to be "intelligent."
Why This Case Is Useful: A vivid, universally relatable hook that reframes an everyday AI interaction as a prediction task — strong opening example for teaching the book's central reframe.
Potential for Reuse: High
```

```
Case Title: Creative Destruction Lab (CDL) and its AI startup portfolio
People / Organization: Creative Destruction Lab (Rotman School, Toronto); portfolio companies including Deep Genomics, Ada, Validere; contributors William Tunstall-Pedoe (Alexa's AI engine) and Barney Pell (formerly led an 85-person NASA team that flew the first AI in deep space)
Context: The authors built CDL, a seed-stage program for science-based startups, which by 2015 was dominated by AI-enabled companies, drawing on Toronto's concentration of foundational machine-learning research (University of Toronto).
What Happened: Deep Genomics predicts what happens in a cell when DNA is altered (medicine); Ada predicts customer intent in online interactions (customer service); Validere predicts the water content of incoming crude oil (oil custody transfer efficiency). The CDL had, **to the authors' knowledge as of September 2022**, the greatest concentration of AI startups of any program on earth over its eight-year history.
Outcome: Presented as a "microcosm of what most businesses will be doing in the near future" — i.e., using prediction to improve an existing workflow.
Concept Illustrated: Prediction as a horizontal input applicable across very different industries (medicine, customer service, energy).
Why This Case Is Useful: Demonstrates the breadth of the prediction lens across dissimilar industries with three concrete, contrasting examples in one paragraph.
Potential for Reuse: Medium — useful as a multi-domain proof point, though each example is only a one-line description in this chapter (more depth may appear later).
```

```
Case Title: University of Toronto's machine learning lineage feeding top industrial AI labs
People / Organization: University of Toronto computer science department alumni; Meta (Facebook), Apple, OpenAI
Context: Cited as part of the authors' explanation for why so many AI leaders were drawn to the Toronto-based CDL.
What Happened: The chapter states that experts formerly in the University of Toronto CS department — where core machine learning inventions were "seeded and nurtured" — now head several of the world's leading industrial AI teams, naming Meta, Apple, and OpenAI specifically.
Outcome: Used as supporting evidence for CDL's credibility and Toronto's centrality to the modern AI field.
Concept Illustrated: Geographic/institutional concentration of foundational AI research talent.
Why This Case Is Useful: A concrete, verifiable-sounding claim (specific companies named) that works well as a factoid or content hook about AI's Toronto origins.
Potential for Reuse: Medium — useful as a supporting fact, though it names outcomes without dates or verifiable sourcing.
```

## 9. Counterintuitive Insights

```
Insight: What looks like "artificial intelligence" in current AI products is actually just prediction — a narrower and more specific capability than general intelligence.
Common Belief: AI systems like Alexa "know" things and are broadly intelligent.
Author's Argument: AI's current capability is a drop in the cost of prediction — using existing information to generate new information about an unknown — not intelligence in a general sense.
Evidence: The Alexa example, plus the claim that this reframing lets economics (a discipline built around understanding trade-offs under uncertainty) explain what AI means for decisions.
Why It Is Surprising: It deflates the "AI = intelligence" narrative dominant in media and business discourse, replacing it with a narrower, more tractable economic concept.
```

## 10. Unique or Unusual Ideas

```
Idea: Applying economics — rather than computer science — as the primary lens for understanding the business implications of AI.
Why It Seems Unique: Most popular AI discourse focuses on technical capability or hype; the authors instead argue that a drop in the cost of an input (prediction) is best analyzed with standard economic cost/trade-off logic, deliberately abstracting away from the underlying technology.
Potential Connection to Other Topics: Cost economics, decision theory, technology adoption
```

## 11. Tensions, Contradictions, and Open Questions

```
Issue: The chapter claims AI "does not actually bring us intelligence but instead a critical component of intelligence—prediction," without yet defining the boundary between "prediction" and "intelligence" precisely.
Author's Position: Prediction is a component of intelligence; the book will use this narrower framing throughout.
Possible Counterargument: One could argue that prediction is intelligence for many practical purposes, or alternatively that other components (judgment, reasoning, action) are equally central and the "just prediction" framing understates what modern AI systems do.
What Evidence Would Help Resolve It: A precise definition distinguishing prediction from intelligence, and evidence on which decision tasks are prediction-bottlenecked versus judgment- or reasoning-bottlenecked (the book's own Chapter 9, "The Value of Judgment," appears designed to address exactly this).
```

Top 5 Concepts: (1) Prediction as an economic input, (2) AI = cheap prediction not general intelligence, (3) Trade-offs as the organizing logic of AI strategy, (4) Economics as the analytical toolkit for a cost-of-input drop, (5) Decision-making under uncertainty as the frame that connects prediction to business outcomes.
