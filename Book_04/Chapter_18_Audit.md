## Missing Items

1. **A missing strategic taxonomy: where the data you need might reside, and what that implies (book p.201)**: This is the most substantive gap. The chapter offers a specific, actionable five-way breakdown of data-location scenarios and their strategic implications that the extraction did not capture as its own framework: "If the data resides with an exclusive or monopoly provider, then you may find yourself at risk of having that provider appropriate the entire value of your AI. If the data resides with competitors, there may be no strategy that would make it worthwhile to procure it from them. If the data resides with consumers, it can be exchanged in return for a better product or higher-quality service. However, in some situations, you and others might have data that can be of mutual value; hence, a data swap may be possible. In other situations, the data may reside with multiple providers, in which case, you might need some more complicated arrangement of purchasing a combination of data and prediction." This is a genuinely useful, actionable strategic checklist — five distinct scenarios (monopoly provider, competitor, consumer, mutual-value swap, multiple providers) each with a different implied strategy — that deserves its own Section 4 framework entry, not just the general "data ownership" concept already captured.
2. **AI predicts the optimal response, not just the weather event itself (book p.194)**: The chapter's capital-impact reasoning is more specific than the extraction conveys: "Suppose that AI allows airlines not only to forecast weather events but to generate predictions for how best to deal with weather-related interruptions." The extraction's Key Claim collapses this into "predicting weather-related contingencies," but the chapter distinguishes two separate predictive capabilities — forecasting the event, and separately predicting the optimal response to it — which is a more precise (and more ambitious) claim about what AI would need to do to justify the contracting-out shift.
3. **The specific threefold reason judgment resists being programmed into a machine (book p.197)**: The chapter gives a more precise mechanism than the extraction's general "hard to specify in a contract" framing: "human judgment, where it is valuable, is utilized because it is difficult to program such judgment into a machine. The rewards are either unstable or unknown, or require human experience to implement." This threefold breakdown (unstable rewards / unknown rewards / requiring human experience) is a more precise causal account of *why* judgment resists automation than the extraction's Key Concept 3 currently states.
4. **The explicit forward-reference to Chapter 19 regarding data-collection investment strategy (book p.198)**: The chapter states directly, in the "Impact of AI: Data" section's opening: "In the next chapter, we explore issues concerning the strategic importance of investing in data collection." The extraction's Section 19 (cross-book summary) doesn't record this specific sentence, even though it's a distinct, useful forward-reference confirming what Chapter 19 will cover (consistent with, and a helpful complement to, the Ch.17 audit's note about Ch.17's own forward references).
5. **The two distinct reasons companies buy advertiser data (book p.199)**: The chapter specifies two distinct motivations that the extraction's advertising case narrative compressed into general language: "Not surprisingly, they buy data that helps them identify high-value customers. They also may buy data that helps them avoid advertising to low-value customers. Both types of data are valuable in that they enable the company to focus its ad spending on high-value customers." These are two distinct data products (positive targeting data vs. negative/exclusion targeting data) worth naming separately, both converging on the same strategic goal (focusing ad spend on high-value customers).
